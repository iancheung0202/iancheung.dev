# Week 6 Study Guide: Surveillance, Numbers, and Decisions

## Big idea

1. Institutions need to **make social order** (control, discipline, security)
2. Surveillance **watches** people so deviations can be spotted and acted on (Lecture 13)
3. Numbers and "objectivity" make those institutions **trusted** by outsiders (Lecture 14)
4. Framing people as rational "decision-makers" lets institutions **steer behavior** with nudges (Lecture 15)
5. None of this is natural. Each is a **historical product** that is co-produced with politics and power
6. Watch for **who is exposed, who is trusted, and who is left out**

## Foucault, "Panopticism" (*Discipline and Punish*)

> **Core idea:** Modern power works less through violence and more by getting people to **police themselves**. Bentham's **Panopticon** is the model.

### Plague town vs. leper
- **Plague town (17th c.)**: town sealed, each street watched by a syndic, daily roll call at windows, everything written down and reported up, everyone fixed in place. This is the **model of discipline** (exceptional, violent, temporary).
- **Leper** = **exclusion** (cast out, binary in/out). Dream of a **pure community**.
- **Plague** = **discipline** (sort, track, individualize everyone). Dream of a **disciplined society**.
- In the 1800s the two merge. Asylums, prisons, schools, and hospitals both **brand** people (normal/abnormal, sane/mad) *and* **individually track and train** them.

### The Panopticon
- Ring of cells around a central tower. The guard sees every cell, but **inmates can't see the guard**.
- Inmate is **seen but doesn't see**: "the object of information, never a subject in communication." Inmates are also cut off from each other (**axial visibility, lateral invisibility**).
- **"Visibility is a trap."**
- Power is **visible but unverifiable**: you always see the tower, but never know if you're being watched right now.
- So you act as if always watched and become **"the principle of your own subjection."**
- **Power is depersonalized.** It doesn't matter who is in the tower. Power lives in the *arrangement* (architecture and lines of sight).
- It's cheap and light (no chains), and **preventative** (acts before any offense).
- It is also a **laboratory**: you can observe, compare, and experiment on people. **Knowledge follows power.**
- It is **generalizable**: prisons, schools, hospitals, factories. It's a *diagram* of power, not just one building. The goal is to make society **more productive**.
- Bentham's "democratic" twist: the public can inspect the tower, so it supposedly can't become tyranny.

**Key terms:** discipline, panopticon, internalized surveillance, normalization, "docile" self-policing

## NYT Opinion: Vasquez, "The N.Y.P.D. Is Teaching America How to Track Everyone Every Day Forever"

> **Core idea:** The NYPD has built a huge, hidden, mostly unregulated surveillance system, and other cities and the federal government are copying it.

- NYPD has spent **$3+ billion**. It tracks via subway data, cameras, **license plate readers**, social media scraping, drones, DNA, iris scans, phone taps, and more.
- Data goes into the **Domain Awareness System**, which keeps it for years and **predicts** where you'll go and who you'll contact.
- For about **90%** of its technologies, NYPD says **no warrant needed**.
- **Gang database**: 13,000+ people, **99% people of color**. Example: a teen in the **Marcy Houses** can be added because of where he lives or who he posts with. Result: repeated stops, hours of questioning, phone seizures (**24,000+ phones in 2024**), undercover cops posing as teens online.
- **Chilling effect**: people avoid protests, change schools, "look over their shoulders." "Paranoia spreads like a contagion."
- **Future-risk example**: abortion is legal in NY but not elsewhere. The data could identify out-of-state visitors to Planned Parenthood, and no law stops the feds from asking for it.
- **Federal level**: DOGE breaking down walls between IRS, SSA, Medicare/Medicaid, and VA data. March executive order told agencies to remove data boundaries. Expanded **Palantir** contracts. USPS reportedly helping DHS track undocumented immigrants.
- State privacy laws all have **carve-outs for law enforcement**. We need to fix this loophole by enacting new laws that ban police from collecting and keeping this data in the first place.

**Connection to Foucault:** same logic (watching changes behavior), but now digital, everywhere, invisible, and **unequal** (differential exposure).

## Porter, "Objectivity and Authority: How French Engineers Reduced Public Utility to Numbers"

> **Core idea:** Numbers became powerful in public life because of **politics**, not just better science. Quantification is a **public rhetoric** for **justifying decisions to outsiders who don't trust you**.

- **Two meanings of objectivity:** **impersonality** (following rules) and **fairness** (impartiality). Numbers give the *appearance* of both. Science is like justice: "supposed to be blind."
- Mechanical processes seem fair because they stand **outside private interests**. Experts should look as "unimaginative" as possible, just applying rules.
- Math is "silent on many points" (it can't express tacit judgment), which is why it's clear and shareable to diverse readers.
- **Who uses numbers?** People under pressure from **powerful outsiders** (public, parliament, rivals). **Secure elites don't need them** because they trust their own judgment.
- Quantification needs **bureaucracy**, and is tied to **democracy**. Numbers are "a tribute to democracy" because personal authority isn't enough.
- **Descriptive statistics** (murders in Belgium) look like plain "facts." **Complex calculations** (is a canal worth building?) mainly **defend** decisions.

### Case: French state engineers (Corps des Ponts et Chaussées)
- Elite engineers from **École Polytechnique**. Math was their special language: **credible to the public but not comprehensible to it**, so it fended off controversy.
- France required a **"declaration of public utility"** before taking land for roads, canals, and railroads. Engineers chose to define it **with numbers**.
- Public works were politically exposed (parliament, prefects, every deputy wanting a connection), so numbers gave protection.
- **Takeaway:** values were being argued *inside* the math. **Standardizing methods meant standardizing values.**
- **Technocracy is not the same as quantification.** French "technocrats" actually trusted **instinct and general culture**. They used numbers only where they **couldn't control** decisions (location and pricing of public works). Postwar **U.S.** was far more mechanical. Expertise "reflects the **embeddedness of science in culture**."

## Lecture 13: Surveillance and Security

> **Core idea:** Surveillance matters more than just privacy. It's a way of making social order, economic power and state-corporate partnerships, and repurposable infrastructure.

**3 narratives**
- **1984:** control the population ("you can't escape")
- **Panopticon:** discipline ("monitor yourself")
- **Global analytics:** security. Find and preempt bad actors, separate signal from noise.

**History:** wartime internal enemies (4th Amendment issue) → 9/11, PATRIOT Act, warrantless NSA surveillance (identify and preempt) → Snowden 2013 (Verizon metadata on 120M subscribers, PRISM).

**Normalized surveillance:** ubiquitous, suspicionless, bulk, cheap, predictive, repurposable. What's new is **precision**.

**Where it comes from:** porous corporation-state boundaries (Flock, Palantir, Anduril, ICE vendors).

**Surveillance culture:** everyday but **differential exposure** (some are targeted, others aren't).

**Alternatives:** individual agency (not enough), contestation, regulation (GDPR Art. 5(c) data minimization).

## Lecture 14: Quantification and Objectivity

> **Core idea:** Trust in numbers is historical. Data is a **rhetorical practice** for persuasion.

**History:** Galileo (primary properties, control), Priestley vs. Lavoisier, census and political arithmetic, double-entry bookkeeping (technology of trust), Condorcet (numbers vs. secrecy and prejudice), rule of law ("judge as automaton," e.g. MLB ABS).

**Porter:**
- Objectivity adapts to the "suspicions of powerful outsiders."
- Quantification is a "technology of distance" that builds trust among strangers.
- It is a "weapon of the weak" where experts lack authority.

**Varieties of objectivity:** truth-to-nature, disciplinary (trust the expert), mechanical (rules/algorithms), intersubjective (agreement).

**Why numbers persuade:** transparent, condensed, commensurable, portable, rule-bound, appear fair.

**Performativity:** metrics change what they measure (U.S. News rankings).

**Takeaway:** objectivity is modern, co-produced with quantification, democracy, and law. It means public, uniform standards instead of personal trust.

## Lecture 15: Making Decisions

> **Core idea:** "Decision-making" is a recent concept (1940s-60s), a **technology and product of history**, not a natural fact. **Agency** is the ability to act, and context shapes who has it.

**Before 1945:** decisions = legal authority (Schmitt); choices = economic behavior (homo economicus).

**Built for WWII logistics and Cold War strategy from:**
1. Cybernetics (Wiener)
2. Behaviorism (Skinner)
3. Game theory (Prisoner's Dilemma)
4. Mind as machine/bureaucracy (Simon, bounded rationality)
5. Behavioral economics (Kahneman and Tversky)

**Choice architecture:**
- **Nudges** and **libertarian paternalism:** you're free to choose, but designers arrange the options.
- **Power = second-order decision-making** (shaping others' choices).
- **Facebook 2010:** voting messages raised turnout.
- **Zuboff:** goal is to change behavior at scale with "data pellets."
- **Hypernudges (Yeung):** decision → data → assessed against goals → architecture reconfigured → nudge again.

**Co-production:** tech and society make each other (avoids technological and social determinism). Models of rational decision ⇄ managerial social order.

**Left out:** relationships, creativity, obligation. AI inherits old assumptions about reasoning.

Equivalence relations

A relation on \(A\) is a subset \(R\subseteq A\times A\); write \(a\sim b\) for \((a,b)\in R\). It is an equivalence relation if for all \(a,b,c\in A\):

  • Reflexive: \(a\sim a\).
  • Symmetric: \(a\sim b\Rightarrow b\sim a\).
  • Transitive: \(a\sim b\) and \(b\sim c\Rightarrow a\sim c\).

Equivalence relations on \(A\) correspond exactly to partitions of \(A\). The class \([a]=\{x: x\sim a\}\) of two elements are equal or disjoint, and the classes cover \(A\).

This is the engine behind cosets, cycles/orbits of a permutation, conjugacy, and orbits of a \(G\)-set.

Prove \(\sim\) is an equivalence relation. Three labelled paragraphs:

  1. Reflexive. Let \(a\in A\). Produce the witness (usually \(e\), \(0\), or \(n=0\)).
  2. Symmetric. Suppose \(a\sim b\); unpack it to get a witness \(x\). Build a witness for \(b\sim a\) (usually \(x^{-1}\) or \(-n\)).
  3. Transitive. Suppose \(a\sim b\) via \(x\) and \(b\sim c\) via \(y\). Combine them (\(yx\), or \(m+n\)).
Three examples

Cosets: \(a\sim_L b\iff a^{-1}b\in H\). Reflexive: \(a^{-1}a=e\in H\). Symmetric: \((a^{-1}b)^{-1}=b^{-1}a\in H\) since \(H\) is closed under inverses. Transitive: \((a^{-1}b)(b^{-1}c)=a^{-1}c\in H\).

Conjugacy (sample Q4a): \(g\sim h\iff \exists x\in G,\ xgx^{-1}=h\). Reflexive: \(x=e\). Symmetric: \(g=x^{-1}h(x^{-1})^{-1}\). Transitive: \(h=xgx^{-1},\,k=yhy^{-1}\Rightarrow k=(yx)g(yx)^{-1}\).

Permutation orbits: \(a\sim b\iff \sigma^n(a)=b\) for some \(n\in\Z\). Reflexive \(n=0\); symmetric \(\sigma^{-n}(b)=a\); transitive \(\sigma^{m+n}(a)=c\).

To disprove, one counterexample is enough: \(x\ge y\) on \(\R\) is not symmetric since \(1\ge 0\) but \(0\not\ge 1\). Notice how each coset property uses a subgroup axiom: \(e\in H\) (reflexive), inverses (symmetric), closure (transitive).

Binary operations & isomorphism

A binary operation on \(S\) is a function \(*:S\times S\to S\): defined for every \(a,b\in S\) and with output in \(S\) (closed). Then \(\langle S,*\rangle\) is a binary algebraic structure. A subset \(H\subseteq S\) inherits an induced operation iff \(H\) is closed under \(*\).

Commutative: \(a*b=b*a\). Associative: \((a*b)*c=a*(b*c)\). Identity: \(e\in S\) with \(e*x=x*e=x\) for all \(x\).

An identity is unique if it exists. A table is symmetric across the main diagonal iff \(*\) is commutative. Composition of functions is always associative (so matrix and permutation products are).

An isomorphism \(\Phi:\langle S,*\rangle\to\langle S',*'\rangle\) is a bijection with \(\Phi(x*y)=\Phi(x)*'\Phi(y)\) for all \(x,y\in S\). Write \(S\cong S'\). Dropping "bijection" gives a homomorphism.

Show \(S\cong S'\).

  1. Define \(\Phi:S\to S'\) by an explicit formula.
  2. Injective: \(\Phi(x)=\Phi(y)\Rightarrow x=y\).
  3. Surjective: given \(x'\in S'\), name \(x\in S\) with \(\Phi(x)=x'\).
  4. Operation preserved: compute \(\Phi(x*y)\) and \(\Phi(x)*'\Phi(y)\); they match.
Examples

\((\R,+)\cong(\R^+,\cdot)\) via \(\Phi(x)=e^x\): injective since \(e^x=e^y\Rightarrow x=\ln e^x=\ln e^y=y\); onto since \(e^{\ln t}=t\); \(e^{x+y}=e^xe^y\).

\((n\Z,+)\cong(\Z,+)\) via \(\Phi(x)=x/n\). Also \(x\mapsto -x\) is an automorphism of \((\Z,+)\) (HW 3.2).

Show \(S\not\cong S'\). Find one structural property that differs (an isomorphism must preserve it): size; commutative or not; associative or not; has an identity; number of solutions of \(x*x=e\); element orders; cyclic or not; subgroup diagram.

If \(\Phi\) is an isomorphism and \(e\) is the identity of \(S\), then \(\Phi(e)\) is the identity of \(S'\) (not "\(e\) itself"). Associativity and commutativity transfer across isomorphisms (HW 3.30: chain \(\Phi(x)*'(\Phi(y)*'\Phi(z))=\Phi(x*(y*z))=\dots\)).

Proving associativity needs all \(a,b,c\). One test case proves nothing (HW 2.2). To disprove, give one triple: \(a*b=ab+1\) on \(\R\) is commutative but not associative, since \((a*b)*c=abc+c+1\) while \(a*(b*c)=abc+a+1\).

Groups

A group is \(\langle G,*\rangle\) with: (1) \(*\) associative; (2) an identity \(e\) with \(e*a=a*e=a\) for all \(a\); (3) each \(a\) has an inverse \(a^{-1}\) with \(a*a^{-1}=a^{-1}*a=e\). It is abelian if also commutative. \(|G|\) is the order.

In a Cayley table: identity gives the first row and column, and every element appears exactly once in each row and column.

In a group: identity and inverses are unique; cancellation (\(ab=ac\Rightarrow b=c\), and \(ba=ca\Rightarrow b=c\)); \(ax=b\) has the unique solution \(x=a^{-1}b\); \((ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}\); \((a^{-1})^{-1}=a\).

Is \(\langle S,*\rangle\) a group? Check in order and stop at the first failure, showing an explicit counterexample.

  1. Closure: is the output always in \(S\)?
  2. Associativity for all triples.
  3. Identity: one \(e\) that works for every element.
  4. Inverses: every element has one.
Sample Q1, worked
  • \(\Q^+\) under division: closed, but not associative (\((8/4)/2=1\ne 4=8/(4/2)\)); \(a/1=a\) but \(1/a\ne a\), so no identity, so no inverses.
  • \(\{\sigma\in S_6:\sigma(2)=4\}\): not closed (\((2\,4)(2\,4)=e\) sends 2 to 2); identity \(e\) is missing; inverse of \((2\,4\,5)\) sends 2 to 5.
  • \(\{\sigma\in S_6:\sigma(2)=2\}\): a group (the stabilizer of 2).
  • \(\Z\) under \(\times\): closed, associative, identity 1; inverses fail (2 has none).

Subgroups

A subset \(H\subseteq G\) is a subgroup, written \(H\le G\), if \(H\) is a group under the operation of \(G\). \(H\lt G\) means proper (\(H\ne G\)). \(\{e\}\) is the trivial subgroup; a nontrivial proper subgroup is neither \(\{e\}\) nor \(G\).

Subgroup test. \(H\le G\) iff (1) \(a,b\in H\Rightarrow ab\in H\); (2) \(e\in H\); (3) \(a\in H\Rightarrow a^{-1}\in H\). Associativity is inherited.

One-step form: \(H\ne\varnothing\) and \(ab^{-1}\in H\) for all \(a,b\in H\). Finite shortcut: a nonempty finite subset closed under the operation is a subgroup (HW 5.50: powers of \(a\) repeat, so \(a^n=e\) and \(a^{-1}=a^{n-1}\)).

Prove \(H\le G\).

  1. Write the defining condition of \(H\).
  2. Check \(e\) satisfies it.
  3. Let \(a,b\in H\); show \(ab\in H\).
  4. Let \(a\in H\); show \(a^{-1}\in H\).
Example (HW 4.18)

\(S=\{M\in GL_n(\R):\det M=\pm1\}\). \(\det I=1\). \(\det(AB)=\det A\det B=\pm1\). \(\det(A^{-1})=1/\det A=\pm1\).

Disprove \(H\le G\). Show one axiom fails: missing identity (\(\Q^+\) in \((\C,+)\): \(0\notin\Q^+\); \(\{M:\det M=2\}\): \(\det I=1\)); not closed (\(\{f:f(1)=1\}\) under \(+\): \((f+g)(1)=2\)); missing inverse.

Subgroups of \((\Z,+)\) are exactly \(n\Z\). \(SL_2(\C)\le GL_2(\C)\). If \(G\) is finite, \(|H|\) divides \(|G|\) (Lagrange, below). The subgroup diagram is a structural invariant.

\(H\) must use the same operation as \(G\) and contain \(G\)'s identity. A subset can be a group under some other operation without being a subgroup.

Cyclic groups

\(\langle a\rangle=\{a^n:n\in\Z\}\), the smallest subgroup containing \(a\) (additively: all multiples \(na\)). \(G\) is cyclic if \(G=\langle a\rangle\) for some \(a\), called a generator. The order of \(a\) is \(|\langle a\rangle|\): the least \(n>0\) with \(a^n=e\), or \(\infty\).

Division algorithm. For \(n\in\Z,\ m\in\Z^+\) there are unique \(q,r\) with \(n=mq+r\), \(0\le r\lt m\).

Every cyclic group is abelian. Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic.

Proof pattern to reuse: let \(m\) be the least positive exponent with \(a^m\in H\). For \(a^n\in H\) write \(n=qm+r\); then \(a^r=(a^m)^{-q}a^n\in H\), so \(r=0\) by minimality, and \(H=\langle a^m\rangle\).

Subgroups of \((\Z,+)\) are exactly \(n\Z\) for \(n\ge0\). For \(r,s\in\Z^+\), \(\{nr+ms\}=d\Z\) where \(d=\gcd(r,s)\); \(d\) divides \(r\) and \(s\), and every common divisor divides \(d\). So \(\langle 18,24,39\rangle=3\Z\) (HW 7.4). If \(\gcd(r,s)=1\) and \(r\mid sm\) then \(r\mid m\).

Classification. If \(G=\langle a\rangle\) is infinite then \(G\cong\Z\); if \(|G|=n\) then \(G\cong\Z_n\).

If \(a\) has order \(n\): \(|\langle a^s\rangle|=n/\gcd(s,n)\), and \(\langle a^s\rangle=\langle a^t\rangle\iff\gcd(s,n)=\gcd(t,n)\). Corollary: the generators of \(\langle a\rangle\) are the \(a^s\) with \(\gcd(s,n)=1\); there are \(\varphi(n)\).

  • In \(\Z_n\), the element \(k\) has order \(n/\gcd(k,n)\); generators are the units.
  • \(\Z_n\) has exactly one subgroup for each positive divisor \(d\mid n\): \(\langle d\rangle\), of order \(n/d\). A cyclic group of order \(n\) has \(\varphi(d)\) elements of order \(d\).
  • \(\Phi:\Z_n\to\Z_n\) is an automorphism iff \(\Phi(1)\) is a generator, so there are \(\varphi(n)\) of them (\(\Z_8\): \(1\mapsto1,3,5,7\); HW 6.14).
  • A homomorphism out of a cyclic group is determined by the image of a generator (HW 6.44).
  • A finite group of order \(n\) is cyclic iff it has an element of order \(n\). Prime order \(\Rightarrow\) cyclic.
  • Not cyclic: \(V_4\), \(\Z_2\times\Z_2\), \((\Q,+)\) (no single element generates both \(1/3\) and \(1/5\)). \((\R,+)\) is not even finitely generated.
\(\Z_{12}\) in full (sample Q7)

Generators: \(1,5,7,11\). Subgroups: \(\langle1\rangle=\Z_{12}\); \(\langle2\rangle=\{0,2,4,6,8,10\}\); \(\langle3\rangle=\{0,3,6,9\}\); \(\langle4\rangle=\{0,4,8\}\); \(\langle6\rangle=\{0,6\}\); \(\langle0\rangle=\{0\}\). Orders \(12,6,4,3,2,1\).

\(\Z_n\) explorer. Check your hand computations.

Additively, \(a^n\) means \(na\). Generators of \(\Z_n\) are the \(k\) with \(\gcd(k,n)=1\) (this includes 1 and \(n-1\), excludes 0); it is not "\(k\) prime".

Generating sets & Cayley graphs

\(\langle a,b\rangle\) is the set of all finite products \(a^{i_1}b^{j_1}a^{i_2}\cdots\) (integer exponents); it is a subgroup. In general \(\langle a_i\mid i\in I\rangle=\bigcap\{H\le G: a_i\in H\ \forall i\}\), the smallest subgroup containing every \(a_i\). They generate \(G\) if this equals \(G\); \(G\) is finitely generated if a finite set generates it.

An intersection of subgroups is a subgroup. Rule: \(a\Z\cap b\Z=\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)\Z\) (e.g. \(4\Z\cap6\Z=12\Z\)). Example: \(\langle2,3\rangle=\Z_6\) since \(\langle2\rangle=\{0,2,4\}\) and \(\langle3\rangle=\{0,3\}\).

The Cayley digraph \(\operatorname{Cay}(G,S)\): vertices are the elements of \(G\); for each \(x\in G\) and generator \(s\in S\) draw an edge \(x\xrightarrow{s}xs\).

Properties (use them to check a drawing): connected because \(S\) generates; each vertex has exactly one outgoing and one incoming edge of each generator type; relations such as \(r^4=e\), \(b^2=e\) hold starting from every vertex. If \(s\) has order 2 then \(x\to xs\to x\), so draw one undirected edge.

Draw \(\operatorname{Cay}(G,S)\).

  1. Tabulate \(x\cdot s\) for every \(x\) and every \(s\in S\).
  2. Place the \(|G|\) vertices.
  3. Draw one edge per \((x,s)\), a different style per generator, arrows only for generators of order above 2.
  4. Check: one in-edge and one out-edge per generator at every vertex.
Sample Q2: \(S_3\) with three generating sets

Products are composed right to left, so \(x\cdot s\) means apply \(s\) first. Pick one convention and stay consistent; the shape of the graph is what matters. Generators of order 2 do not need arrows.

Permutations

A permutation of a set \(A\) is a bijection \(A\to A\). \(S_A\) is the set of all of them, a group under composition (\(\sigma\tau=\sigma\circ\tau\): apply \(\tau\) first). \(S_n=S_{\{1,\dots,n\}}\) has order \(n!\). \(S_1\) is trivial, \(S_2\cong\Z_2\), \(S_3\) is the smallest non-abelian group (order 6). If \(|A|=|B|\) then \(S_A\cong S_B\).

\(\sigma\) induces the equivalence relation \(a\sim b\iff\sigma^n(a)=b\) for some \(n\in\Z\). Its classes are the orbits (cycles). The cycle \((a_1\,a_2\,\dots\,a_k)\) sends \(a_i\mapsto a_{i+1}\), \(a_k\mapsto a_1\), and fixes everything else.

Every permutation of a finite set is a unique product of disjoint cycles; disjoint cycles commute. Every permutation is a product of transpositions (not uniquely): \((a_1\,\dots\,a_k)=(a_1\,a_k)\cdots(a_1\,a_3)(a_1\,a_2)\), so a \(k\)-cycle is \(k-1\) transpositions.

Parity is well defined: no permutation is both a product of an even number and of an odd number of transpositions. \(A_n\) (even permutations) is a subgroup of \(S_n\) of order \(n!/2\) for \(n\ge2\). \(\Phi:S_n\to\Z_2\) (even \(\mapsto0\), odd \(\mapsto1\)) is a homomorphism.

  • Order of a permutation = lcm of its disjoint cycle lengths. A \(k\)-cycle is even iff \(k\) is odd.
  • Inverse: reverse each cycle, \((a_1\,a_2\,a_3)^{-1}=(a_3\,a_2\,a_1)\).
  • Write cycles starting from the smallest entry; \((1\,3\,6)=(3\,6\,1)\ne(1\,6\,3)\).
  • \(D_n\) (symmetries of a regular \(n\)-gon) has order \(2n\): \(r^k\) and \(sr^k\), with \(r^n=s^2=e\), \(srs=r^{-1}\). \(D_4\) has 8 elements and is non-abelian; its center is \(\{e,r^2\}\) (Fraleigh: \(\rho_0,\rho_2\)).
  • Subgroups of \(D_4\): order 1 \(\{e\}\); order 2 \(\langle r^2\rangle,\langle s\rangle,\langle sr\rangle,\langle sr^2\rangle,\langle sr^3\rangle\); order 4 \(\langle r\rangle\), \(\{e,r^2,s,sr^2\}\), \(\{e,r^2,sr,sr^3\}\); order 8 itself.

Compute products, powers, order, parity.

  1. Product: trace each number right to left, then collect cycles.
  2. Power of a \(k\)-cycle: \(c^m\) moves each entry \(m\) places along; reduce \(m\bmod k\). For several cycles, reduce \(m\) modulo the order.
  3. Parity: sum of \((\text{length}-1)\) over cycles, mod 2.
Examples

\((1\,3)(1\,4\,2)\): \(1\to4\to4\), \(4\to2\to2\), \(2\to1\to3\), \(3\to3\to1\), so \((1\,4\,2\,3)\).

HW 4.8: \(\sigma\) a 6-cycle, \(100=6\cdot16+4\), so \(\sigma^{100}=\sigma^4\).

Order of \(((1\,4\,2\,3),2)\in S_4\times\Z_3\): \(\operatorname{lcm}(4,3)=12\).

Permutation calculator. Type a product in cycle notation, e.g. (1 3)(1 4 2). Rightmost cycle acts first.

Image of a homomorphism. \(\Phi[G]=\{\Phi(g):g\in G\}\) is just the set of outputs \(\Phi\) actually hits.

  • It is a subgroup: if \(\Phi:G\to G'\) is a homomorphism, then \(\Phi[G]\le G'\).
  • If \(\Phi\) is also injective, then \(G\cong\Phi[G]\): \(G\) sits inside \(G'\) as an exact, undistorted copy (an embedding), like a line inside 3-space.

Why it is a subgroup: it contains \(\Phi(e)=e'\); it is closed since \(\Phi(a)\Phi(b)=\Phi(ab)\); it has inverses since \(\Phi(a)^{-1}=\Phi(a^{-1})\). Why \(G\cong\Phi[G]\): restrict the target to \(\Phi[G]\) so the map is automatically onto; if it is also one-to-one, it is a bijection, hence an isomorphism.

Cayley's theorem. Every group \(G\) is isomorphic to a subgroup of \(S_G\). HW 8.52: \(p_a(x)=xa\) is a permutation of \(G\), and \(a\mapsto p_{a^{-1}}\) is the isomorphism.

Right-to-left is the course convention; reading left to right gives a different (inverse-order) product. Disjoint cycles commute; overlapping ones usually do not.

Cosets & Lagrange

Coset = a shifted copy of \(H\). For \(H\le G\) and \(a\in G\): left coset \(aH=\{ah:h\in H\}\), right coset \(Ha=\{ha:h\in H\}\). Additively, \(a+H\). If \(G\) is abelian, \(aH=Ha\).

Picture \(H\) as a line through the origin; \(a+H\) is a parallel line.

Why it works: \(a\sim_L b\iff a^{-1}b\in H\) ("\(a\) and \(b\) differ by an element of \(H\)"). Like "mod 5": \(a\sim b\iff b-a\in5\Z\). Since \(\sim_L\) is an equivalence relation, its classes (the cosets) cut \(G\) into non-overlapping pieces.

Coset rules.

  • \(aH=bH\iff a^{-1}b\in H\) (additively: \(b-a\in H\)).
  • \(aH=H\iff a\in H\) (shifting by something already in \(H\) changes nothing).
  • Cosets partition \(G\): every element lies in exactly one coset; two cosets are equal or disjoint.
  • \(aH\) is a subgroup only if \(a\in H\) (otherwise it misses \(e\)).

Example. \(G=\Z,\ H=3\Z\). Three cosets: \(0+H=\{\dots,-3,0,3,6,\dots\}\), \(1+H=\{\dots,-2,1,4,7,\dots\}\), \(2+H=\{\dots,-1,2,5,8,\dots\}\). They cover \(\Z\) with no overlap, and \(1+H=4+H\) since \(4-1=3\in H\). Only \(0+H\) is a subgroup.

Index \((G:H)\) = number of distinct left cosets. \((\Z:3\Z)=3\). For finite \(G\): \((G:H)=|G|/|H|\).

Lagrange's theorem. If \(G\) is finite and \(H\le G\), then \(|H|\) divides \(|G|\).

Why: every coset has exactly \(|H|\) elements (\(h\mapsto ah\) is a bijection, undone by \(x\mapsto a^{-1}x\)). Cosets partition \(G\) into \((G:H)\) buckets of size \(|H|\), so \(|G|=(G:H)\cdot|H|\). A group of 12 elements can never have a subgroup of size 5 or 7.

Corollaries: (1) prime order \(\Rightarrow\) cyclic, and the only subgroups are \(\{e\}\) and \(G\); (2) \(|a|\) divides \(|G|\); (3) \(a^{|G|}=e\) for all \(a\).

Why (3): with \(k=|a|\) and \(|G|=km\), \(a^{|G|}=(a^k)^m=e\). This generalizes Fermat's little theorem.

List all left cosets of \(H\) in \(G\).

  1. Count: \(|G|/|H|\) cosets, each of size \(|H|\).
  2. Start with \(eH=H\). Pick any element not yet used, \(g\), and compute \(gH=\{gh\}\).
  3. Repeat until every element of \(G\) appears exactly once.
  4. For normality: compare \(gH\) with \(Hg\) for a suspicious \(g\).
Sample Q11: \(H=\langle(1\,2\,3)\rangle\le A_4\)

\(|A_4|=12\), \(|H|=3\), so 4 cosets: \(H\); \((12)(34)H=\{(12)(34),(243),(143)\}\); \((13)(24)H=\{(13)(24),(142),(234)\}\); \((14)(23)H\). Not normal: with \(g=(14)(23)\), \((123)g=(142)\in Hg\) but \((142)\notin gH\), so \(gH\ne Hg\).

Lagrange as an impossibility tool. If \(\Phi:G\to G'\) is injective, then \(G\cong\Phi[G]\le G'\), so \(|G|\) must divide \(|G'|\).

Sample Q10: injective \(S_4\to D_{100}\)?

\(|S_4|=24\), \(|D_{100}|=200\), and \(24\nmid200\). No.

Classic proof patterns. (HW 10.34) If \(|G|=pq\) with \(p,q\) prime, a proper subgroup has order 1, \(p\) or \(q\), so it is cyclic. (HW 11.31) If \(G\) has no proper nontrivial subgroups: any \(x\ne e\) gives \(\langle x\rangle=G\), so \(G\) is cyclic. If infinite, \(\langle x^2\rangle\) is proper nontrivial, a contradiction. If \(|G|=ab\) with \(1\lt a,b\lt|G|\), then \(\langle x^a\rangle\) is proper nontrivial. So \(|G|\) is prime.

The converse of Lagrange is false: \(A_4\) (order 12) has no subgroup of order 6. It does hold for abelian groups. Left and right cosets differ unless \(H\) is normal.

\(\Z_n^\times=\{i:1\le i\le n-1,\ \gcd(i,n)=1\}\) is a group under multiplication mod \(n\). Fermat: \(a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\) for \(a\in\{1,\dots,p-1\}\) (Lagrange applied to \(\Z_p^\times\)).

Direct products & abelian groups

Direct product. \(G_1\times\cdots\times G_n\) uses componentwise operation. Identity \((e_1,\dots,e_n)\); order \(|G_1|\cdots|G_n|\).

Order of an element = lcm, not product: \(\ord(a_1,\dots,a_n)=\operatorname{lcm}(|a_1|,\dots,|a_n|)\).

When is a product cyclic? \(\Z_m\times\Z_n\cong\Z_{mn}\iff\gcd(m,n)=1\) (also for several pairwise coprime factors).

\(\Z_2\times\Z_2\) is not cyclic: every element has order at most \(\operatorname{lcm}(2,2)=2\), so nothing reaches order 4.

FTFGAG (finite case). Every finite abelian group is a product of prime-power cyclic groups \(\Z_{p^k}\), and this list is unique up to order. Every finitely generated abelian group is that times some copies of \(\Z\).

Counting. Factor \(n=p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_s^{a_s}\). The number of abelian groups of order \(n\) is \(p(a_1)\cdots p(a_s)\), where \(p(a)\) = ways to write \(a\) as a sum of positive integers: \(p(1)=1,\ p(2)=2,\ p(3)=3,\ p(4)=5\).

Existence. If \(m\) divides \(|G|\) for finite abelian \(G\), there is a subgroup of order \(m\). Square-free order \(\Rightarrow\) cyclic.

Count or list abelian groups of order \(n\).

  1. Factor \(n\) into prime powers.
  2. For each \(p^a\), split the exponent \(a\) into a sum (a partition); each split gives a product of \(\Z_{p^{\text{part}}}\).
  3. Multiply the number of choices across primes.
Order 100 (sample Q6) and 360

\(100=2^2\cdot5^2\): \(2\cdot2=4\) groups. For \(2^2\): \(2\) (as \(\Z_4\)) or \(1+1\) (as \(\Z_2\times\Z_2\)). Same for \(5^2\). So \(\Z_4\times\Z_{25}\), \(\Z_2^2\times\Z_{25}\), \(\Z_4\times\Z_5^2\), \(\Z_2^2\times\Z_5^2\).

\(360=2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5\): \(3\cdot2\cdot1=6\) groups. (\(2^3\): \(3\), \(2+1\), \(1+1+1\); \(3^2\): \(2\), \(1+1\); \(5\): \(1\).)

Are two finite abelian groups isomorphic? Break both fully into prime-power pieces (use \(\Z_{mn}\cong\Z_m\times\Z_n\) for coprime \(m,n\)). They are isomorphic iff they have exactly the same pieces.

HW 11.16: \(\Z_2\times\Z_{12}\cong\Z_2\times\Z_3\times\Z_4\cong\Z_4\times\Z_6\), since all give \(\{\Z_2,\Z_4,\Z_3\}\).

Abelian groups of order \(n\).

Internal direct product (HW 11.50). In \(G=H\times K\), view \(\bar H=H\times\{e\}\) and \(\bar K=\{e\}\times K\) as subgroups of \(G\):

  • every \(g\in G\) is \(hk\) with \(h\in\bar H,\ k\in\bar K\);
  • elements of \(\bar H\) commute with elements of \(\bar K\);
  • \(\bar H\cap\bar K=\{(e,e)\}\).

Order of a tuple is the lcm, not the product. \(\Z_2\times\Z_2\) and \(\Z_2\times\Z_4\) are not cyclic; \(\Z_4\times\Z_2\not\cong\Z_8\). In \(\Z_n\), \(|a|=n/\gcd(a,n)\).

Homomorphisms & normal subgroups

Homomorphism \(\Phi:G\to G'\): a map that respects the operation, \(\Phi(ab)=\Phi(a)\Phi(b)\) (multiply then map = map then multiply).

Trivial: \(\Phi(g)=e'\) for all \(g\). Isomorphism: bijective homomorphism. Automorphism: isomorphism \(G\to G\).

Homomorphisms: sign \(S_n\to\Z_2\) (even \(\mapsto0\), odd \(\mapsto1\)); evaluation \(f\mapsto f(c)\) on \((F,+)\); \(v\mapsto Av\) from \(\R^n\to\R^m\); \(\det:GL_n(\R)\to\R^*\); \(n\mapsto rn\) on \(\Z\); reduction \(\Z\to\Z_n\); inclusion \(H\hookrightarrow G\); conjugation \(x\mapsto gxg^{-1}\).

Not homomorphisms:

  • \(n\mapsto n+c\) with \(c\ne0\) on \(\Z\): it sends \(0\) to \(c\ne0\), and \(\Phi(n+m)=n+m+c\) but \(\Phi(n)+\Phi(m)=n+m+2c\).
  • \(x\mapsto x^{-1}\) on a non-abelian group (HW 13.8): \(\Phi(ab)=b^{-1}a^{-1}\) but \(\Phi(a)\Phi(b)=a^{-1}b^{-1}\). It is a homomorphism iff \(G\) is abelian.

Image, preimage, kernel. \(f[A]=\{f(a):a\in A\}\) (outputs). \(f^{-1}[B]=\{x:f(x)\in B\}\) (inputs landing in \(B\)).

Kernel \(\Ker\Phi=\{g\in G:\Phi(g)=e'\}\): everything that gets squashed to the identity.

Properties of a homomorphism \(\Phi:G\to G'\):

  • \(\Phi(e)=e'\) and \(\Phi(a^{-1})=\Phi(a)^{-1}\).
  • Subgroups map to subgroups: \(H\le G\Rightarrow\Phi[H]\le G'\). Preimages too: \(K'\le G'\Rightarrow\Phi^{-1}[K']\le G\).
  • \(\Phi(a)^n=\Phi(a^n)\), so \(|\Phi(a)|\) divides \(|a|\). Isomorphisms preserve element orders exactly.
  • Compositions of homomorphisms are homomorphisms (HW 13.49).

Normal subgroup \(H\nrm G\): left and right cosets agree, \(gH=Hg\) for all \(g\in G\). Equivalent: \(gHg^{-1}=H\), or \(ghg^{-1}\in H\) for all \(g\in G,\ h\in H\) (closed under conjugation).

Automatically normal: any subgroup of an abelian group; any subgroup of index 2; any kernel; \(A_n\nrm S_n\).

\(\Ker\Phi\nrm G\). Injectivity test: \(\Phi\) is injective iff \(\Ker\Phi=\{e\}\) (just solve \(\Phi(g)=e'\)).

Fibres are cosets: all inputs sent to \(\Phi(a)\) form the coset \(a\Ker\Phi\).

\(\Phi[G]\) is abelian iff \(xyx^{-1}y^{-1}\in\Ker\Phi\) for all \(x,y\) (HW 13.50). Index 2 \(\Rightarrow\) normal.

Normal does not mean elements of \(H\) commute with elements of \(G\). It only means conjugates stay in \(H\): \(ghg^{-1}\in H\), not \(ghg^{-1}=h\). \(gH=Hg\) is equality of sets. "Trivial kernel \(\Leftrightarrow\) injective" holds only for homomorphisms, not general functions.

Quotient groups & the FHT

Quotient group. \(G/H=\{aH:a\in G\}\) (the set of left cosets) with \((aH)(bH)=(ab)H\). This product is well defined iff \(H\nrm G\).

Then \(G/H\) is a group: order \((G:H)\); identity \(H\); \((aH)^{-1}=a^{-1}H\); \((aH)^n=a^nH\).

Natural map. \(\gamma:G\to G/H\), \(\gamma(x)=xH\), is onto with kernel \(H\). So every normal subgroup is the kernel of some homomorphism.

Fundamental Homomorphism Theorem (FHT). If \(\Phi:G\to G'\) is a homomorphism with kernel \(H\), then \(G/\Ker\Phi\cong\Phi[G]\), via \(\mu(gH)=\Phi(g)\). Also \(\Phi=\mu\circ\gamma\).

Conjugation. The conjugate of \(x\) by \(g\) is \(gxg^{-1}\). The map \(\iota_g(x)=gxg^{-1}\) is an automorphism of \(G\) (inner). HW 14.27: \(H\sim K\iff gHg^{-1}=K\) for some \(g\) is an equivalence relation (reflexive \(g=e\); symmetric \(g^{-1}\); transitive \(hg\)).

If \(H\nrm G\) has finite index \(m\), then \(a^m\in H\) for all \(a\in G\) (HW 14.30): in \(G/H\), \((aH)^m=H\) because \(|G/H|=m\).

Prove \(G/H\cong K\).

  1. Define a map \(\Phi:G\to K\) and show it is a homomorphism.
  2. Show \(\Phi\) is onto \(K\).
  3. Compute \(\Ker\Phi\) and show it equals \(H\) exactly.
  4. Conclude by FHT.

Identify an abstract finite abelian \(G/H\).

  1. Order: \(|G|/|H|\).
  2. Order of a coset \(aH\): the smallest \(n>0\) with \(a^n\in H\).
  3. If some coset has order \(|G/H|\), the quotient is cyclic. Otherwise match element orders against the candidates (e.g. \(\Z_4\) vs \(\Z_2\times\Z_2\)).
Examples

HW 14.12: in \(\Z_4\times\Z_4\) with \(H=\langle(1,1)\rangle\): \((3,1)\notin H\) but \(2(3,1)=(2,2)\in H\), so \((3,1)+H\) has order 2.

\(\Z_2\times\Z_4\) mod \(\langle(1,2)\rangle=\{(0,0),(1,2)\}\): order 4. For \((0,1)\): \(2(0,1)=(0,2)\notin H\), \(4(0,1)=(0,0)\in H\), so order 4. The quotient is \(\cong\Z_4\).

Shortcut when \(H=\langle(1,x,y)\rangle\): a coordinate equal to 1 can be "killed" by \((a,b,c)\mapsto(b-xa,\,c-ya)\) (onto, kernel \(H\)). E.g. \((\Z_4\times\Z_4\times\Z_8)/\langle(1,2,4)\rangle\cong\Z_4\times\Z_8\).

Sample Q8: why \(aH\cdot bH=abH\) fails for non-normal \(H\). Some \(g\in G,\ h\in H\) have \(g^{-1}hg\notin H\). Then \(hH=eH\), yet \((hH)(gH)=hgH\) while \((eH)(gH)=gH\), and \(hgH=gH\iff g^{-1}hg\in H\), which is false. The answer depends on which representative you pick, so the product is not well defined.

Also: \(G/H\times H\) need not be \(\cong G\) (\(\Z_4/\langle2\rangle\times\langle2\rangle\cong\Z_2\times\Z_2\ne\Z_4\)), and two quotients of the same order can differ (\(\Z_2\times\Z_4\): \(G/\langle(1,0)\rangle\cong\Z_4\) but \(G/\langle(0,2)\rangle\cong\Z_2\times\Z_2\)).

Simple groups, center, normalizers

Simple group (the "atoms"): \(G\ne\{e\}\) whose only normal subgroups are \(\{e\}\) and \(G\). It cannot be broken into smaller quotients, like a prime number.

Facts: \(\Z_p\) (\(p\) prime) are the only simple abelian groups. \(A_n\) is simple for \(n\ge5\).

Maximal normal subgroup: normal \(M\lt G\) with no normal \(N\) strictly between (\(M\lt N\lt G\)). \(M\) is maximal normal \(\iff G/M\) is simple.

Homomorphisms and normality. If \(N\nrm G\), then \(\Phi[N]\nrm\Phi[G]\). If \(N'\nrm\Phi[G]\), then \(\Phi^{-1}[N']\nrm G\).

Center \(Z(G)=\{z:zg=gz\ \forall g\in G\}\): the elements that commute with everything. It is a normal subgroup, and \(G\) is abelian iff \(Z(G)=G\).

Commutator \([a,b]=aba^{-1}b^{-1}\) measures failure to commute: \([a,b]=e\iff ab=ba\).

Commutator subgroup \(C=\langle aba^{-1}b^{-1}\mid a,b\in G\rangle\) is normal. \(G/C\) is abelian (the abelianization: crush all the non-commutativity). \(G/N\) is abelian iff \(C\le N\).

For \(D_4\) (symmetries of a square): \(Z=C=\{e,r^2\}\), the 180° rotation.

Normalizer \(N_G(S)=\{g\in G:gSg^{-1}=S\}\): the elements that leave the set \(S\) fixed (as a whole) under conjugation. \(H\nrm G\iff N_G(H)=G\).

Show \(N_G(S)\le G\) (sample Q5). Write \(N=N_G(S)\).

  1. \(eSe^{-1}=S\), so \(e\in N\).
  2. \(g,h\in N\): \((gh)S(gh)^{-1}=g(hSh^{-1})g^{-1}=gSg^{-1}=S\), so \(gh\in N\).

Image and preimage of a normal subgroup (Section 15 templates).

  • \(\Phi[N]\nrm\Phi[G]\): take \(y=\Phi(g)\in\Phi[G]\), \(x=\Phi(n)\in\Phi[N]\). Then \(yxy^{-1}=\Phi(gng^{-1})\in\Phi[N]\) since \(gng^{-1}\in N\).
  • \(\Phi^{-1}[N']\nrm G\): take \(g\in G\) and \(h\) with \(\Phi(h)\in N'\). Then \(\Phi(ghg^{-1})=\Phi(g)\Phi(h)\Phi(g)^{-1}\in N'\) because \(N'\) is normal.

Decide abelian / cyclic / simple (sample Q3).

  • Abelian: one non-commuting pair kills it; otherwise cite a known abelian group.
  • Cyclic: must be abelian; find a generator, or use \(\Z_m\times\Z_n\) cyclic iff \(\gcd=1\).
  • Simple: an abelian group is simple iff its order is prime. A non-abelian group is not simple if you find a nontrivial proper normal subgroup (index 2, a kernel, \(V_4\) in \(A_4\), \(A_n\) in \(S_n\)). \(A_n\) with \(n\ge5\) is simple.

Group actions

Group action. A rule letting \(G\) move the points of a set \(X\): \((g,x)\mapsto gx\), with

  • \(ex=x\) (identity does nothing);
  • \((g_1g_2)x=g_1(g_2x)\) (compatible with the group operation).

\(X\) is then a \(G\)-set. Each \(g\) acts as a permutation \(\sigma_g(x)=gx\) of \(X\), and \(g\mapsto\sigma_g\) is a homomorphism \(G\to S_X\).

Examples: \(GL_n(\R)\) on \(\R^n\) (\(Av\)); \(D_n\) on the vertices of an \(n\)-gon; \(S_n\) or \(A_n\) on \(\{1,\dots,n\}\); \(G\) on itself by \(g\cdot g'=gg'\); \(G\) on \(G/H\) by \(g(aH)=(ga)H\); trivial action \(gx=x\); \(\R^*\) scaling \(\R^n\); any \(H\le S_X\) on \(X\).

Types of action.

TermPlain meaningCondition
FaithfulDifferent group elements do different things; only \(e\) leaves everything alone.\(gx=x\ \forall x\Rightarrow g=e\)
TransitiveYou can get from any point to any other. One single orbit.\(\forall x_1,x_2\ \exists g:\ gx_1=x_2\)
FreeA non-identity element never fixes any point.\(gx=x\) for some \(x\Rightarrow g=e\)

For \(g\in G\) and \(x\in X\):

  • Fixed points of \(g\): \(X_g=\{x:gx=x\}\subseteq X\).
  • Stabilizer of \(x\): \(G_x=\{g:gx=x\}\le G\) (a subgroup: \(e\) fixes \(x\); \(g,h\) fix \(x\Rightarrow gh\) does; \(g^{-1}x=g^{-1}(gx)=x\)).
  • Orbit of \(x\): \(Gx=\{gx:g\in G\}\subseteq X\) (everywhere \(x\) can be sent).

\(x_1\sim x_2\iff\exists g,\ gx_1=x_2\) is an equivalence relation. Its classes are the orbits, so \(X\) is a disjoint union of orbits. Conversely, a disjoint union of \(G\)-sets is a \(G\)-set (Section 16).

Orbit–Stabilizer. \(|Gx|=(G:G_x)\). For finite \(G\): \(|Gx|\cdot|G_x|=|G|\). So every orbit size divides \(|G|\).

Implications. Free \(\Rightarrow\) faithful (if \(X\ne\varnothing\)): an element fixing every point fixes some point, so it is \(e\). Faithful does not imply free. Free means every stabilizer is trivial, so every orbit has full size \(|G|\). \(G\) acting on itself by left multiplication is free and transitive.

Read an action table (sample Q12). Rows are points \(x_j\), columns are group elements \(g_i\), entries are \(g_ix_j\).

  1. Stabilizer \(G_x\): in row \(x\), the columns whose entry equals \(x\).
  2. Orbit \(Gx\): the set of entries in row \(x\).
  3. Check \(|Gx|\cdot|G_x|=|G|\) for each \(x\) you computed.
  4. Free: every stabilizer is \(\{e\}\). Transitive: one orbit. Faithful: no column other than \(e\) fixes every row (equivalently, all columns are distinct).
  5. Non-abelian: find \(g,h,x\) with \(g(hx)\ne h(gx)\).
  6. \(G\cong S_n\): restrict to an orbit \(O\) of size \(n\); \(g\mapsto\sigma_g|_O\) is a homomorphism. If injective and \(|G|=n!\), it is an isomorphism.

Give an example, or show none exists (sample Q9).

  • Free and transitive: \(G\) on itself by left multiplication.
  • Faithful, not free: \(S_3\) on \(\{1,2,3\}\) (the transposition \((1\,2)\) fixes 3).
  • Free, not transitive: \(\Z_2\) swapping pairs of \(\{1,2,3,4\}\) via \((1\,2)(3\,4)\).
  • Free, not faithful: impossible, since free implies faithful.

The lecture writes both the stabilizer and the orbit as \(Gx\). Keep \(G_x\le G\) (stabilizer, a subgroup) separate from \(Gx\subseteq X\) (orbit, a subset). Burnside's lemma is not in the notes.

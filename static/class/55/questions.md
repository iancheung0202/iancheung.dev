**(22 Spring) Let $p$ be a prime number $\ge3$ and let $n$ be a positive integer. Prove that if $x$ is an integer such that $x^2-1$ is divisible by $p^n$, then either $x-1$ or $x+1$ is divisible by $p^n$. Is the analogous statement true if $p=2$?**
> Given that $p^n \mid (x^2 - 1)$. Factoring the difference of squares, we have $p^n \mid (x - 1)(x + 1)$. Since $p$ is a prime factor of $p^n$, $p$ must divide the product $(x-1)(x+1)$. By Euclid's Lemma (if a prime divides a product, it divides at least one factor), we know that either $p \mid (x-1)$ or $p \mid (x+1)$. **Case 1:** Assume $p \mid (x-1)$. We can express $x+1$ as $(x-1) + 2$. Since $p \mid (x-1)$, if $p$ were to also divide $(x+1)$, then $p$ would have to divide their difference: $(x+1) - (x-1) = 2$. However, we are given that $p \ge 3$, so $p$ cannot divide 2. Therefore, $p \nmid (x+1)$. Since $p$ is the only prime factor of $p^n$ and $p$ does not divide $x+1$, we have $\gcd(p^n, x+1) = 1$. We now apply the generalized Euclid's Lemma: If $a \mid bc$ and $\gcd(a, c) = 1$, then $a \mid b$. With $a=p^n$, $b=(x-1)$, and $c=(x+1)$, we have $p^n \mid (x-1)(x+1)$ and $\gcd(p^n, x+1)=1$. Therefore, $p^n \mid (x-1)$. **Case 2: Assume $p \mid (x+1)$.** Similarly, write $x-1 = (x+1) - 2$. Since $p \nmid 2$, $p$ cannot divide $x-1$. Thus, $\gcd(p^n, x-1) = 1$. By the generalized Euclid's Lemma, since $p^n \mid (x-1)(x+1)$ and $\gcd(p^n, x-1) = 1$, it follows that $p^n \mid (x+1)$. In either case, one of the factors must be divisible by $p^n$. **Is it true for $p=2$?** No. Counterexample: Let $p=2$, $n=3$, and $x=3$. $x^2 - 1 = 8$, which is divisible by $p^n = 2^3 = 8$. However, $x-1 = 2$ (not divisible by 8) and $x+1 = 4$ (not divisible by 8). The proof fails because $p=2$ does divide the difference between the factors (which is 2), so $p$ can (and does) divide both $x-1$ and $x+1$, splitting the factors of $p^n$ between them.

**(22 Fall) Prove or disprove: if $x$ is a rational number and $x^2$ is an integer, then $x$ must be an integer.**
> Let $x \in \mathbb{Q}$. We can write $x = \frac{a}{b}$ where $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}, b \ne 0$, and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ (lowest terms). We are given $x^2 = k$ where $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. $\frac{a^2}{b^2} = k \implies a^2 = k b^2$. Suppose $b > 1$. Then by the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, there exists a prime $p$ such that $p \mid b$. If $p \mid b$, then $p \mid b^2$, and since $a^2 = k b^2$, $p \mid a^2$. If a prime divides a square, it must divide the root, so $p \mid a$. However, we stated that $\gcd(a, b) = 1$. It is a contradiction for $p$ to divide both $a$ and $b$. Therefore, $b$ cannot be greater than 1. Since $b$ must be a positive integer (standard representation of rationals), $b=1$. Thus $x = \frac{a}{1} = a$, which is an integer.

**(21 Spring) Prove that if $r$, $s$, and $n$ are positive integers, and $r^2=s^2 n$, then $n$ is a perfect square. Then, use your result to prove that if $n$ is a positive integer and not a perfect square, then $\sqrt{n}$ is irrational.**
> **Part 1:** Given $r^2 = s^2 n$, we can rearrange to $n = \frac{r^2}{s^2} = \left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^2$. Since $n$ is an integer, $\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^2$ is an integer. Let $x = \frac{r}{s}$. Since $x$ is rational and $x^2$ is an integer, by the proof in the previous question (22 Fall), $x$ must be an integer. Let $k = \frac{r}{s}$ where $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then $n = k^2$. Thus, $n$ is a perfect square. **Part 2:** Assume $\sqrt{n}$ is rational. Then $\sqrt{n} = \frac{r}{s}$ for integers $r, s$. Squaring both sides: $n = \frac{r^2}{s^2} \implies r^2 = s^2 n$. By the result in Part 1, this implies $n$ is a perfect square. Therefore, if $n$ is not a perfect square, $\sqrt{n}$ cannot be rational (it must be irrational).

**(19 Spring) If $A$, $B$, and $C$ are sets such that $A\cap C=B\cap C$ and $A\cup C=B\cup C$, does it follow that $A=B$?**
> We will show that $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq A$. Let $x$ be an arbitrary element of $A$. **Case 1: $x \in C$.** If $x \in A$ and $x \in C$, then $x \in A \cap C$. Since $A \cap C = B \cap C$, then $x \in B \cap C$, which implies $x \in B$. **Case 2: $x \notin C$.** If $x \in A$, then clearly $x \in A \cup C$. Since $A \cup C = B \cup C$, then $x \in B \cup C$. Because $x \in B \cup C$ but $x \notin C$, it must be that $x \in B$. In both cases, $x \in A \implies x \in B$, so $A \subseteq B$. The argument is symmetric for $B \subseteq A$. Thus, $A=B$.

**(16 Spring) Prove that $A$ and its power set $\mathcal{P}{(A)}$ cannot have the same cardinality.**
> We assume for the sake of contradiction that $|A| = |\mathcal{P}(A)|$. This implies there exists a bijection $f: A \to \mathcal{P}(A)$. Consider the set $S = \{x \in A : x \notin f(x)\}$. Since $S$ is a subset of $A$, $S \in \mathcal{P}(A)$. Because $f$ is a bijection (specifically surjective), there must exist some element $c \in A$ such that $f(c) = S$. **If $c \in S$**, by the definition of $S$, $c \notin f(c)$. Since $f(c) = S$, this means $c \notin S$. (Contradiction). **If $c \notin S$**, by the definition of $S$, $c \in f(c)$. Since $f(c) = S$, this means $c \in S$. (Contradiction). Both cases lead to a contradiction. Therefore, no such bijection $f$ exists. Since $|\mathcal{P}(A)| > |A|$ (trivial for finite sets, true for infinite), they cannot have the same cardinality.

**(14 Spring) Suppose that $f:A\rightarrow \mathcal{P}{(A)}$ is a function from a set to its power set. Let $B=\{x\in A:x\notin f(x)\}$, and let $c$ be an element of $A$. Show that $f(c)\ne B$ by deriving a contradiction from the assumption $f(c)=B$.**
> Let $B = \{x \in A : x \notin f(x)\}$. Assume there exists $c \in A$ such that $f(c) = B$. We check if $c$ is in $B$: $c \in B \iff c \in \{x \in A : x \notin f(x)\} \iff c \notin f(c)$. Substitute $f(c) = B$ gives us $c \in B \iff c \notin B$. This is a contradiction ($P \iff \neg P$ is always false). Therefore, the assumption $f(c) = B$ must be false.

**(21 Spring) Let $G$ be a graph constructed by arranging $n$ vertices around a circle and connecting every pair by an edge, except for pairs of vertices that are next to each other on the circle. For which values of $n$ does this graph have an Euler circuit?**
> $n$ must be odd (and $n \ge 5$). The graph described is the Complete Graph $K_n$ with the edges of the Cycle Graph $C_n$ removed. The degree of every vertex in $K_n$ is $n-1$. The degree of every vertex in $C_n$ is 2. The degree of every vertex in $G$ is $(n-1) - 2 = n-3$. For a graph to have an Euler circuit, every vertex must have an even degree (and the graph must be connected). Therefore, $n-3 \text{ is even} \iff n \text{ is odd}$.

**(19 Spring) For which values of $n$ does the complete graph on $n$ vertices have an Euler circuit?**
> $n$ is odd. In a complete graph $K_n$, every vertex is connected to every other vertex. The degree of every vertex is $n-1$. For an Euler circuit to exist, the degree of every vertex must be even. Therefore, $n-1 \text{ is even} \implies n \text{ is odd}$.

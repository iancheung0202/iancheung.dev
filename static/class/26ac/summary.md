## Unit Wrap-Up: Introduction

### Core Foundations of the Unit
Throughout this unit, we established key concepts and frameworks necessary for analyzing music in this course:

1. **Music & Culture** — Music is culturally constructed, not universal.
2. **Musical Elements** — Shared vocabulary for describing sound (rhythm, pitch, timbre, texture, form).
3. **Recording Technology** — Technology shapes how music is produced, preserved, circulated, and understood.
### Master Narratives and History
- Master narratives determine whose stories are told and valued.
- Takaki’s concept of the U.S. master narrative:
  - Dominant story: Americans are of European ancestry.
  - Raises the question: **Who gets to be seen as “American”?**
  - U.S. history always included people of diverse origins.

**Takaki’s Multiculturalism**
- Structural and representational practices recognizing multiple racial/cultural groups.
- Encourages thinking beyond a Black/White racial binary.
- Highlights that dominant narratives omit crucial experiences of other groups.
- A fuller view of U.S. history allows deeper mutual understanding.

**Important Reminder**
- History telling is subjective.
- Who tells the story shapes what is included, excluded, and valued.
### Music as Culturally Constructed
- Musical meaning is produced by people within cultural contexts.
- Music does not inherently “mean” something.
- Identity and music shape each other—music expresses and constructs identity simultaneously.
### Musical Elements
To discuss music critically, we rely on shared analytical terminology. Refer to Lecture Highlights for full musical vocabulary.
### Technology, Mediation & Musical Meaning
Technology is never neutral; it shapes what music becomes.

**Critical Questions**
- Is recording a form of documentation or creativity?
- What counts as “authentic”?
- How does technology reshape musical practices?
- Who has access to technology? What gets preserved, and what is lost?
### Looking Forward
These foundational concepts guide our analysis throughout the course:
- Music is culturally constructed.
- Identity and music co-construct each other.
- Master narratives shape what stories get told.
- Technology mediates and transforms musical meaning.

American music is unified yet deeply diverse—full of contradictions that help us understand U.S. culture.

## Unit Wrap-Up: Migration, Music & Identity – Lecture Highlights

### Overview
Across this unit, we examined musical traditions shaped by migration:
- Anglo American Ballads  
- Mexican American Corridos  
- African American Spirituals  
- French Louisiana Music (Cajun & Zydeco)

Despite unique histories and sounds, shared themes connect them.
### Key Themes

#### 1. Migration Creates Musical Traditions
Movement—forced, voluntary, or internal—creates conditions for new music.
Music travels easily because it is portable and orally transmitted.

**Examples**
- **Forced Migration:** Spirituals (transatlantic slavery), Cajun music (Acadian expulsion).
- **Voluntary Migration:** Anglo ballads (British settlers), corridos (borderlands movement).
- **Internal Migration:** Zydeco (Louisiana → Houston during the Great Migration).
#### 2. Oral Tradition & Cultural Memory
Oral transmission enables both continuity and change.

**Examples**
- **Ballads:** Variations from localization, censorship, memory.
- **Corridos:** Adapt structure to document current events.
- **Spirituals:** Passed through invisible churches and Black congregations.
- **Cajun/Zydeco:** Learned in families and communities.

**Critical Question:**  
What happens when oral tradition encounters recording technology?
#### 3. Technology, Mediation & “Authenticity”
Recording preserves, but also transforms traditions.

**Early Preservation**
- Child (ballad texts), Bronson (melodies), Library of Congress field recordings.

**Commercial Technology**
- 3-minute recording formats shape song structure.
- Corrido field trips; localized zydeco recording industry (45s/tapes).
- Cost and access affect what gets recorded.

**Electric Amplification**
- Zydeco modernizes into urban soundscape (guitar, bass, drums).

**Contemporary Mediation**
- Videotaping as community practice (1980s zydeco).
- National TV visibility (Buckwheat Zydeco).
#### 4. Documentation & Authority
Who documents music determines how it is represented.

**Academic Collectors**
- Child, Sharp, Campbell, White focused on “authentic,” isolated communities.
- “Folk = authenticity” became an ideological stance.
- Communities labeled “primitive” to justify collection.

**Implications**
- Oral ballads valued more than broadside ballads.
- But both served meaningful purposes in their communities.

**Alternative Documentation**
- Community-run zydeco recording and video.
- Musicians as tradition-bearers rather than scholars.
#### 5. Music as Community & Identity
Music binds communities, preserves memory, and carries identity.

**Examples**
- Ballads: communal Appalachian singing.
- Corridos: narrate community events.
- Spirituals: collective worship, ring shout.
- Zydeco: dance music sustaining Creole identity.

Music acts as a bridge between past and present.
#### 6. Resistance & Counternarrative
Marginalized communities use music to resist dominant narratives.

**Examples**
- Spirituals: coded communication (“Steal Away”), metaphorical critique.
- Corridos: heroes challenging Anglo authority (Gregorio Cortez).
- African reinterpretation of Christianity through cultural lens.
- Cajun/French preservation despite legal language suppression.

## Unit Wrap-Up: Race and the Popular Music Industry – Lecture Highlights

### Major Themes

#### 1. Genre as Social Construct
- Genres are invented by the industry to market to specific audiences.
- These labels shape who gets heard, valued, and paid.
#### 2. The Modern Music Industry
- Tin Pan Alley consolidates music publishing (1880s), excluding African Americans.
- Recording + publishing → modern industry that shapes what music becomes.
- Mamie Smith’s *Crazy Blues* (1920) = start of Race Records.
- Revealed a pre-existing Black audience the industry had ignored.

**Copyright and Power**
- Copyright protected melody/lyrics (written tradition), disadvantaging oral musics.
- “Little Bird Told Me” case: arrangements aren't copyrightable → rise of cover versions.
#### 3. From Hillbilly to Bluegrass
- "Hillbilly" marketed to rural white southerners (1920s).
- Rebranded “country music” in 1940s.
- Bill Monroe → bluegrass defined as distinct genre.
- 1960s folk revival elevates bluegrass as “authentic folk” despite commercial roots.
#### 4. Musical Appropriation & Cover Versions
- Rock ’n’ roll = marketing term aimed at white teens.
- Cover versions allowed white performers to profit from Black innovations.

**Case Study: “Hound Dog”**
- Written by white songwriters stereotyping Black idioms.
- Performed by Big Mama Thornton (1953).
- Popularized by Elvis Presley (1956).
- Meaning, tempo, and perspective changed dramatically.

**Critical Questions**
- How does music change when separated from originators?
- Who profits? Who is erased?
#### 5. Technology Shapes Musical Meaning
**MTV (1981) and Racial Exclusion**
- Initially promoted white rock musicians.
- Black artists largely denied access.

**Michael Jackson’s *Thriller***
- Broke racial barriers on MTV.
- Elevated music video to cinematic art.

**Hollywood Film Music**
- *Jazz Singer* (1927): technological milestone + blackface.
- Max Steiner → Hollywood orchestra as global “superculture.”
- Musical topics (heroic brass, exotic percussion, romantic strings) encode ideology.
- Foley shows that “recording produces the feeling of the real.”

## Unit Wrap-Up: The Cultural Work of Discourse and Practices – Lecture Highlights

### Major Themes

#### 1. Historiography: Who Writes Music History?
- Historiography constructs meaning; history is not neutral.
- Official jazz history is gendered—mostly stories of men.

**Pellegrinelli’s Argument**
- Jazz origins narrated through gendered parentage:
  - Blues = feminine (voice, emotion, body)
  - Ragtime = masculine (skill, piano, training)
- Singing disappears from narratives to elevate jazz as “art.”

**WWII and Women Musicians**
- “Swing Shift Maisies”: women fill roles but framed as temporary.
- Expertise overshadowed by gendered performance expectations.
#### 2. Music Criticism: Constructing Legitimacy
- Critics shape meaning through judgments tied to race/class/gender/region.
- Meaning cannot be understood through sound alone—requires discourse.

**Lester Bangs & “The Party”**
- Defines rock’s essence as wild, physical, anti-intellectual.
- Detroit becomes symbol of authentic American rock aesthetic.
- MC5 (political) vs. Stooges (foolish) as competing “Parties.”

Criticism defines not only genres but also places and identities.
#### 3. Gender, Embodiment, and Community in Tango
- Tango emerges from immigrant communities → becomes national symbol.
- Milonga codes reinforce gender roles.

**Kathy Davis**
- Embodied passion cannot be explained solely through feminist critique.
- Feminist tensions should be embraced as “affective dissonance.”
- Tango provides experiences of gendered difference otherwise unavailable.

**Piazzolla’s New Tango**
- Concert-oriented, dissonant, influenced by classical music.
- Helped create the 1980s Tango Renaissance.
#### 4. Challenging Dominant Narratives: Asian American Jazz Movement
- Normative jazz discourse = Black/White binary.
- Asian Americans excluded through racial stereotypes.

**The Movement**
- Bay Area musicians → blend of politics + music.
- Institutions: Asian Improv Records, Asian Improv Arts, Asian American Orchestra.
- Musical innovations combine Asian instruments/styles with free jazz.

**Fred Ho**
- Critiques jazz as exclusionary.
- Advocates for “Asian American Music.”

**Significance**
- Expands jazz history beyond racial binary.
- Creates political/cultural space for marginalized voices.
- Challenges essentialist notions of authenticity.

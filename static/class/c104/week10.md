# Week 10 Study Guide: The Tech Workplace

## Big idea

1. Tech culture is **made**, not natural. It is a set of <u>norms, scripts, and projections</u> that teach people how to act, who to be, and what to want.
2. The culture **fits some people and not others**. It naturalizes familiar forms of power around gender, race, and immigration status.
3. The **founder mythology** of disruption and heroic individualism is the opposite of the <u>carrier bag</u> *(maintenance and ongoing care)*.
4. The workplace is **tiered**. It rewards technical skill at the top and hides <u>invisible labor</u> at the bottom.
5. **Positionality matters.** People who don't fit are often best placed to see how the system works, and an <u>intersectional</u> lens shows barriers that diversity numbers miss.
6. **Individual agency inside the system is hard**, and companies usually revert to old practices. Change may need <u>collective action</u>.

## Lecture 25

> **Core idea:** Silicon Valley culture is a coherent set of norms, scripts, and projections. It fits certain people, naturalizes certain kinds of power, and is hard to change from inside.

- **Founder mythology** = innovation, experimentation, optimism, disruption, breakage. Move fast and break things.
- **Making culture** happens through <u>reward structures</u>, <u>hierarchies and role models</u>, and <u>managing selves</u>: normalized practices, identities, and mottos.
- **Workplace scripts:** venerate founders, hustle and be 10x, hit KPIs, climb or strike out on your own, dream of IPO or acquihire.
- **Who counts as a tech worker?** Founders and back-end engineers at the top, then data scientists and PMs, then HR and marketing, then contractors, crowdworkers, drivers, and cafeteria staff. A **tiered workplace** that rewards technical skill and hides **invisible labor**.
- **Reward structures:** salary, equity, options, perks, and an ecosystem that celebrates founders. Free movement of labor and low unionization.
- **Managing selves at the system level:** who is in or out, recruiting for <u>fit</u>, **homophily** reinforcing groups, and how that shapes the workforce.
- **Dreams and realities.** Culture is a recruiting draw and part of compensation, which compensates for precarity, layoffs, and the fear of becoming out of date.
- **The view from below.** The Valley also means wage stagnation for non-tech workers, toxic waste, and a workforce without women or people of color. People who don't fit may understand the system best.
- **Modes of agency, first pass:** in-house teams, lawsuits, whistleblowing. The pattern question: what actually changes?

## Lecture 26

> **Core idea:** Who fits in the tech workplace is shaped by gender, race, and immigration status together. Diversity efforts come and go, so change may take collective rather than individual agency.

- **Women in tech.** Women were present early, on the original Macintosh team and as a cofounder of Cisco, but the share of women in computing has fallen since. A large share report negative career outcomes tied to being a woman.
- **2020 DEI surge.** A wave of commitments focused on hiring and leadership, driven by <u>metrics and targets</u> rather than cultural change. It was a patchwork, and after 2020 companies cut DEI teams.
- **Latinx workers** face entry barriers rooted in education and resource gaps, underrepresentation in leadership, limited VC access, and pressure to adopt a generic global identity.
- **African American workers** face a long history of anti-Black racism, high attrition especially for Black women, and **social closure**: exclusion from networks, mentorship, and internal social capital.
- **Indian workers** are **techno-braceros**: tech lobbied to expand H-1B caps, creating a pipeline of foreign workers whose visa status binds them to employers. This is <u>legal violence</u>, and layoffs or policy shifts leave them exposed.
- **Intersectionality** brings hidden barriers into view. Companies adapt temporarily to criticism and then revert, because the structures are resilient. Culture that feels natural to some is hostile to others.
- **Modes of agency:** gathering within through groups like Techqueria, lawsuits like Ellen Pao's against Kleiner Perkins, whistleblowing by Frances Haugen and others, and in-house ethics teams like Timnit Gebru's and Rumman Chowdhury's, which were later dismantled. Each raises the same question: what actually changes?
- **Collective action.** The 2018 **Google Walkout** over a $90M payout to Andy Rubin demanded an end to forced arbitration, pay equity, and an employee board seat. Organizers faced retaliation. It led to the **Alphabet Workers Union** in 2021 and groups like the Tech Workers Coalition.

## Wiener, "Uncanny Valley"

> **Core idea:** A first-person account of a woman in a non-engineering role at a San Francisco startup. The essay shows tech culture from the inside: how it recruits, rewards, and shapes selves, and who it quietly excludes.

- **Culture as identity.** The team frets that its culture is dying and tells itself that <u>we are the company</u>. Work takes over identity, and the promised IPO becomes a <u>deus ex machina</u> (everything will be magically fixed) that justifies everything.
- **Perks as compensation.** Kombucha on tap, ping-pong, and off-sites read like summer camp. Perks and culture substitute for stability.
- **Hierarchy of skill.** Engineers sit at the top and <u>non-engineers must prove their value</u>. Her promotion depends on learning to code, and not wanting to feels like a moral failure. The firm believes in **meritocracy** and bootstrapping.
- **Gender.** Almost every woman at the company cries in the bathroom at some point. A coworker greets the first woman engineer by predicting everyone will hit on her, and she is the only engineer without server access. Her mother's advice: don't put complaints about sexism in writing.
- **Money and complicity.** Conversations circle money without specifics, friends become overnight millionaires, and investors talk about Oakland as a place to buy but not live. She is disgusted by her own complicity in the city's change.
- **Managing selves.** Morale is treated as a <u>break/fix problem</u>: a scavenger hunt, a loyalty meeting asking whether she is down for the cause. She realizes later she was reading from someone else's script and quits.

## Baker, "The Stanford Freshmen Who Want to Rule the World"

> **Core idea:** Venture capitalists now recruit 18-year-olds. A Stanford inside Stanford turns students into founders through access and hype, and the <u>meritocracy story</u> hides a system that runs on connections and rewards recklessness.

- **Stanford as incubator with dorms.** VCs camp at campus cafes, employ student <u>talent scouts</u>, and hand out <u>pre-idea funding</u>. The university earns rent from companies on its land and runs its own fund, StartX.
- **Access over skill.** The inner circle is invite-only and decided by who you know and which accelerator you did. Insiders admit it is just vibes.
- **The pressure to say yes.** Amber Yang was pursued from her first week and struggled to separate what she wanted from how people saw her. She later rejects the idea that the Valley is meritocratic.
- **The dropout myth.** Hennessy says the most successful startups came from graduate students, not dropouts, and young founder-CEOs with too much power produce failures like **Theranos** and Clinkle.
- **Fraud and funding co-develop.** Students confess to fraud and still get funded. Steve Blank, who invented the lean startup method, says the Valley has lost its moral compass and founders now follow the money rather than core beliefs.
- **VCs mostly guess.** About 2% of firms earn 95% of returns, and the herd moves from VR to crypto to AI. The model needs a few huge wins, so hype is rational.

## How it connects

- **Wiener and Baker are the same culture at two stages.** Baker shows how VCs recruit and shape founders before they have ideas. Wiener shows what the resulting workplace feels like for someone outside the engineer-founder mold.
- **Meritocracy is the shared myth.** Baker's students, Wiener's coworkers, and the DEI metrics all assume talent rises on its own, while access, fit, and homophily do the sorting.
- **Back to Week 9.** The founder mythology and free movement of labor that built the Valley are the same forces that make its workplace exclusionary and resistant to change.

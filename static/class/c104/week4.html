Week 4 Study Guide: How Was the World Datafied?

🧵 Big idea

  1. States needed to govern people and territory
  2. So they had to count and classify people
  3. This created "populations" as an idea
  4. Statistics (science of the state) was invented to describe those populations
  5. Those same statistical tools got hijacked by eugenics
  6. And after WWII, this same logic went global (UN, IMF, development, fertility control)
  7. What data we leave out and whose interests it encodes just reshapes the reality it claims to just describe (performativity).

📘 Lecture 8: Populations and States

Core idea: States can't govern what they can't see. To govern, they simplify people into countable categories. This creates "the population" as a concept that didn't exist before.

  • Sovereignty through knowledge: knowing your subjects = a form of power. Foucault's biopower: managing human life itself as a resource for political power.
  • "Statistics" literally means "science of the state" (German Statistik). In English it was called "political arithmetic(k)" (William Petty, John Graunt, late 1600s).
  • John Graunt's "Table of Casualties": counted deaths in 17th-c. London. Turned unique individual deaths into an abstract, aggregate "population." First move from individuals → statistical patterns.
  • The "administrative grid": states impose simplified categories (like Pennsylvania's surveyed grid, or census categories) onto messy reality.
  • Performativity: describing/classifying/measuring the world also changes reality to fit the categories.
  • U.S. Census (1790): first count sorted people into: free white males (16+ and <16), free white females, other free persons, slaves. Shows how categories reflect power/priorities of whoever's counting (race, gender, freedom status all baked in from day one).
  • Quetelet's "Average Man" / social physics (1830s): Belgian statistician applied the normal (bell) curve to human traits (height, chest size, crime rates). Created the idea of "normality": an ideal type, with deviation = "abnormal"/"pathological."

📗 Lecture 9: Eugenics and Statistics

Core idea: Modern statistics (regression, correlation, significance testing) was built by eugenicists specifically to argue that races/classes differ biologically and that "good breeding" should be socially engineered.

  • Francis Galton (1822-1911) — Darwin's half-cousin. Coined "nature vs. nurture." Wrote Hereditary Genius (1869), including a chapter "The Comparative Worth of Different Races" — openly racist.
    • Invented regression and correlation (from studying parent/child heights). Originally called "reversion towards mediocrity" — later renamed "regression to the mean."
    • Coined the term eugenics (1883): "the science... that improves the inborn qualities of a race."
    • Took Quetelet's bell curve and applied it to social class + "genetic worth."
  • Karl Pearson (1857-1936) — founded mathematical statistics as a discipline (chi-squared test, correlation coefficient). Extremely racist ("struggle of race with race... survival of the fitter race"). Invented significance testing to argue some populations were biologically distinct/superior.
  • Ronald Fisher (1890-1962) — invented much of modern stats vocabulary (p-values, ANOVA, F-test, "significance," "null hypothesis"). Lifelong eugenicist; supported sterilization of the "feeble-minded"; defended Nazi geneticists after WWII.
  • Stanford — it's first president David Starr Jordan + Lewis Terman (Stanford-Binet IQ test) were eugenicists; this legacy runs into Silicon Valley "pronatalism"/embryo-selection today (see Wired/WSJ articles in slides).
  • Why it still matters (not that stats = evil):
    • History helps us denaturalize assumptions baked into tools we still use (e.g., significance testing was built to prove group differences existed, not to explain why or how much).
    • Helps us see who's excluded/harmed by metrics that may sound "objective" but are actually shaped by the same biases and power structures that informed the original eugenicist thinking.

📙 Lecture 10: Taking the Global View

Core idea: After WWII, the same "view from above" that states used on their own populations got scaled up to a global view through the UN, IMF, World Bank, etc. "The global" is invented during mid-20th-century as a word/concept.

  • National → global administrative grid: standardized categories, processes, sampling/estimation methods, indices (CPI, GDP) now scaled up.
  • Who else uses these tools besides states? Insurance companies, political pollsters, marketers.
  • What made "the global" emerge (post-WWII):
    1. Cold War (First/Second/Third World framing)
    2. Decolonization (new independent nations, "Global South," non-aligned movement)
    3. Economic globalization (flows of goods/capital/labor, multinational corporations, "development")
    4. New international institutions like UN, World Bank, IMF, mostly created by/for the Global North, producing data "in service of international management and development."
  • Case study: Gapminder.org: a visualization for global population/fertility/health data. It questions whose narrative does "global data" tell, and whose does it leave out?
  • Population control narratives:
    • Fear of a "population explosion" → framed poor women (esp. women of color in the Global South) as the "problem," fertility management as "the solution."
    • This ignored the real drivers (capital flows, wages, jobs, migration, environment/pollution).
    • Continues colonial power relations by turning some women into "experimental subjects" and economic resources to be managed by elites.
    • Shift over time from 1950s-60s "population control" language to 1990s-2000s now reframed as "women's empowerment" and family planning as individual choice.
  • Representing people as data involves a core tension:
    • 👍: It simplifies complex information so systems and institutions can function.
    • 👎: It can feel unfair, incomplete, and agency-denying to the people being modeled.

📖 James Scott, Seeing Like a State, Ch. 1 "Nature and Space"

Core idea (the "parable"): Scott uses the history of scientific forestry in 18th-century Prussia/Saxony as a metaphor for how states (and other powerful institutions) understand and manipulate complex reality.

  • States/officials couldn't see a forest — they could only see "commercial wood": a narrow fiscal abstraction (revenue-yield-per-acre). Everything else (fodder, medicine, habitat, local uses) fell outside the frame — it was "bracketed."
  • Foresters eventually didn't just simplify their description of the forest — they physically re-engineered real forests into neat single-species, same-age rows (monoculture) to match their tables and match the "Normalbaum" (standardized/model tree). Simplification became a tool of control, not just measurement.
  • Short-term this worked great (easy to count, manage, harvest) — long-term it caused ecological collapse (Waldsterben, "forest death": soil depletion, pest outbreaks, storm vulnerability) because so much of the real complexity had been ignored/bracketed out.
  • Scott then draws the exact same logic to taxation and measurement: pre-modern states couldn't tax effectively because local measures were wildly inconsistent (different regions, different "bushels," etc.) — this was "illegible" to the state.
  • The metric system (post-French Revolution) is presented as the parallel case to scientific forestry: a standardizing, simplifying technology imposed from the center that:
    • Helped commerce/trade (uniform units → easier long-distance transactions)
    • Helped the state tax/administer more precisely
    • Was tied to Enlightenment ideals of "the same law, same measures, same rules" for all citizens — i.e., simplification wasn't only about control, it was bundled with an emancipatory promise of equal citizenship
    • Took a very long time to actually replace local practice (people kept using old units informally for decades)

Reading Question 1: What makes German forestry similar to the metric system? What's the point of the comparison?

Both are cases of the state imposing a single, simplified, uniform standard on a messy, locally varied reality, in order to make that reality legible, countable, and controllable from the center — and both did so by literally reshaping reality to fit the standard (real forests became monoculture grids; local measurement customs were forcibly replaced by the meter). The point of the comparison is that this is a general logic of state power, not a coincidence specific to forests — the same "simplify → standardize → impose → reshape reality" pattern shows up again and again wherever states try to govern (forests, taxation, land, and — as Lectures 8/9 show — populations). It's Scott's way of saying: this is how states see, period.

Reading Question 2: What does Scott mean by "legibility"? Legible to whom, for what?

Legibility = making something readable/measurable/manageable from a distance, by a central authority, using simplified categories (tables, maps, grids, standard units) instead of engaging with the full, messy, local complexity of the thing itself. Something is illegible when it exists only in dense, local, particular knowledge that outsiders (state officials) can't parse or act on.

  • Legible to the state — to bureaucrats, tax collectors, planners sitting in an office who need to "read" an entire forest/kingdom/population at a glance via tables rather than by knowing it on the ground.
  • For the purpose of taxation, revenue extraction, military conscription, political control — i.e., legibility exists to serve state interests, not to fully or accurately describe reality. It's always a partial, interested view — "a state's eye view."
  • Key twist: legibility isn't just passive description — it becomes performative (same idea as Lecture 8!): once the state's simplified categories have real power/law/coercion behind them, they start reshaping reality to actually match the grid (real forests became monocultures; French citizens really did become "the same" under uniform law/measures).

📖 Aubrey Clayton, "How Eugenics Shaped Statistics" (Nautilus)

Core idea: The three "founders" of modern statistics — Galton, Pearson, Fisher — were not neutral scientists who happened to also hold eugenicist views; eugenics was the actual motivating purpose behind many of their key statistical inventions.

  • Galton: invented regression/correlation, popularized the bell curve for human traits; called Africans "lazy, palavering savages"; wanted a eugenic "Jehad" (his word) against customs that let "unfit" people breed.
  • Pearson: invented the chi-squared test and the basic 3-step template of significance testing (1. hypothesize a distribution/null hypothesis, 2. measure deviation with a test statistic, 3. get a p-value to decide "significance") — developed specifically to argue that distinct races/skull types existed and differed in worth. Praised the "slaughter" of Native Americans as progress. Founded Biometrika and the Annals of Eugenics journal (still exists today, renamed).
  • Fisher: wrote the textbook (Statistical Methods for Research Workers, 1925) that made significance testing the industry standard across all of science; also pushed for sterilization of the "feeble-minded" and defended Nazi geneticists after WWII (Hitler called Galton's eugenics tradition his inspiration/"my bible" via Madison Grant).
  • Central argument: significance testing only asks "does a difference/effect exist at all?" (yes/no) — not "how much," "why," or "does it matter." That "sizeless" yes/no framing was exactly what eugenicists needed (they just needed to prove two "races" were statistically distinct — didn't care about magnitude or cause). This flaw is still baked into how science uses p-values today (see: the ASA's 2016 statement "don't say statistically significant," Meehl & Lykken's famous 1968 finding that with enough data, everything becomes "significant").
  • The myth of objectivity: Pearson/Fisher insisted they were just "letting the data speak," which made their conclusions seem unquestionable — but this claimed neutrality was itself a rhetorical/political strategy that hid their agenda.
  • Bottom line (not "throw out statistics"): we can't fully separate a tool from the history/interests that shaped it. The fix isn't to pretend stats is pure math untouched by bias — it's to be honest that statistics is "a human enterprise subject to human desire, prejudice, consensus, and interpretation," and to actively work to diversify and rebuild the field with that honesty in mind.

Reading Question 3: How should we think about statistics, given this history? What does this help us do (since we're not going to stop using stats)?

We should stop treating statistics as a purely neutral, objective, "view from nowhere" tool, and instead recognize it as a human practice shaped by the interests and worldviews of the people who built it. This doesn't mean statistics is "ruined" or useless — it means:

  • We should be suspicious of any claim to pure objectivity (that's exactly the rhetorical move Pearson/Fisher used to make their racist conclusions look unquestionable).
  • We should ask, for any statistical method or metric: what was it originally built to prove? What does it silently assume? What does "significant" or "normal" actually mean here, and for whom?
  • We should focus on magnitude, mechanism, and context ("how much," "why," "does it matter") rather than just binary existence claims ("is there a difference, yes/no") — because the yes/no framing is the eugenicist legacy specifically.
  • We should push for a more inclusive, self-aware, "decolonized" statistics — diversifying who does statistics and whose questions get asked — as part of fixing, not abandoning, the discipline.
  • In short: use the tools, but hold them at arm's length; don't let "the math says so" become an excuse to stop thinking critically about power, history, and who benefits.

📖 Reading 10 — Alastair Brooks, "Why Are Certain Countries Poor?" (2024)

(Slightly different topic — comparative/relational thinking about global development, but same underlying critique of taking "objective" global metrics at face value.)

  • HDI (Human Development Index) combines life expectancy, education, income — better than GDP alone, but still distorted (e.g., Ireland ranked #2 in the world in 2020 mainly because of tax-haven-inflated corporate income, not real living standards).
  • Critiques the "ladder of development" model (Walt Rostow, 1960): the idea that poor countries are just "behind" rich ones on the same historical ladder, and will eventually "catch up" if they follow the same path the West did (traditional society → take-off → high mass consumption).
    • Problem: this ignores that rich countries' wealth was built through colonialism/extraction from poor countries. Poor countries aren't "50 years behind" — they are poor because of the same shared global history that made rich countries rich (their poverty and others' wealth are relationally connected, not separate timelines).
  • "Prisoners of geography" / environmental determinism critique: physical geography doesn't doom nations to poverty (Florida, Hong Kong, Netherlands all thrived despite "bad" geography) — political/economic relationships matter more.
  • Cultural comparison as a tool of subjugation: framing colonized people as "backward" (vs. "civilized" colonizers) ignored that their labor was what enabled the colonizer's "modern" economy in the first place.
  • Case study: Sierra Leone vs. UK healthcare — instead of framing Sierra Leone's hospitals as "stuck in the past" (like UK hospitals 100 years ago), Brooks argues this is a "relational comparison": today's Sierra Leone healthcare crisis is actively caused by the same global system that benefits the UK — e.g., the "medical brain drain" (Sierra Leonean-trained doctors/nurses emigrating to work in the NHS) simultaneously worsens Sierra Leone's healthcare and improves the UK's.
  • Main takeaway: Instead of comparing countries as if they're isolated units at different points on the same timeline ("developing" vs "developed," "behind" vs "ahead"), we should use relational comparison — looking at how countries' fortunes are causally connected through trade, colonialism, labor migration, and global institutions (WTO, capital flows, debt).

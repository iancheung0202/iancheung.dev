Week 3 Study Guide: Making Robust Knowledge & Science in Society

Lecture 6: Making Robust Knowledge

Core question: What makes scientific knowledge "robust" (solid, trustworthy)?

Three philosophies of science, in order of sophistication

  1. Logical positivism (1920s–60s): Science starts from pure empirical data; theory is just data organized by logic. Theories get "verified" by more data (induction).

    • Problem: You can never prove a generalization true just by piling up confirming cases — the classic "black swan problem" (seeing 1,000 white swans never proves "all swans are white"; one black swan refutes it).

  2. Falsificationism (Karl Popper, 1930s–70s): Since theories can't be verified, a good theory is one that could in principle be falsified — and hasn't been yet. Science progresses by bold conjectures and ruthless attempts to refute them, not by confirming what we already believe.

    • Problem: Real scientists don't actually abandon theories the moment one contrary data point appears. Max Planck's quip: science doesn't progress because opponents change their minds — it progresses because they eventually die and a new generation takes over.

  3. The deeper problem — underdetermination: Any single hypothesis is never tested alone; it's tested along with a whole web of background assumptions (the Duhem-Quine thesis). If an experiment fails, you don't know which piece of the web to blame — the theory itself, or an auxiliary assumption. This leads to:

    • Underdetermination of theory by data: the data alone are never enough to force you to pick one theory over its rivals — multiple theories can fit the same data. The real problem in science is not "what do the facts say" but "how do we choose between theories that all fit the facts."
    • Related idea: theory-dependence (theory-ladenness) of observation — what counts as "data" already reflects theoretical assumptions; scientists don't approach data with a blank, neutral mind.

Kuhn shifted the conversation from logic to sociology/history — from asking "what's the correct method?" to asking "what do scientists actually do?"

  • Paradigm: Kuhn's central and famously slippery term. A paradigm is not just a theory — it's the whole package a scientific community shares:

    • a landmark achievement/exemplar (a model solved problem, like Newton's Principia) that defines what "good" work looks like
    • shared theories, laws, and concepts
    • shared instruments and techniques
    • shared standards for what counts as a legitimate question and a legitimate solution
    • In short: a paradigm is both a worldview and a form of scientific life/practice.

  • Normal science: Once a field has a paradigm, most scientific work is puzzle-solving within that framework, not questioning the framework itself. Puzzles have expected solutions; if you fail, the assumption is you did something wrong, not that the paradigm is broken. Normal science is taught to students through training on the shared exemplars.

  • Crisis and revolution (paradigm shift): Anomalies (persistent unsolved puzzles) accumulate until they trigger a crisis. A new paradigm is proposed; eventually the community converts to it. This is a revolution, not a smooth accumulation of facts.

  • Incommensurability: Rival paradigms are hard to compare directly because:

    • they disagree on which problems even need solving (different standards for what counts as a solved problem)
    • key terms change meaning across paradigms (e.g., "mass" in Newton vs. Einstein; "earth" as fixed vs. moving)
    • practitioners of different paradigms, in a sense, "live in different worlds" — they don't see the same things as significant when looking at the same phenomena
    • Because of this, paradigm choice can't be settled by pure logic/proof alone. It's more like a conversion experience (Kuhn even compares it to a gestalt switch — like the classic duck-rabbit image) — helped along by demonstrated problem-solving success, but also by aesthetic appeal (elegance, simplicity) and even generational turnover, echoing Planck's remark above.

  • Scientific community: Knowledge isn't produced or certified by lone geniuses; it's produced and judged collectively by an institutionalized community of trained experts who share standards. This is why Kuhn's view is social, not just logical.

  • Case study — the Copernican Revolution: Ptolemaic astronomy (earth-centered, using complex "epicycles" to fit planetary motion) vs. Copernican astronomy (sun-centered, simpler orbits). This is Kuhn's go-to example of a paradigm shift: it wasn't that new data suddenly proved Copernicus right — Copernicus's theory wasn't even more accurate at first — it was a whole new way of seeing the problem that eventually won over the community.

Is this "just relativism"? Kuhn says no — it's not "anything goes." The scientific community (through things like peer review) still collectively arbitrates what counts as good work; there's just no perspective-free, single "scientific method" standing outside all communities to judge them.

Takeaways: Look beyond the data alone. Knowledge is made through the social, intersubjective work of a community, working within a historical context — you need to look at both the content (theories, data) and the context (community, institutions, history) of how knowledge gets made.

Reading: Kuhn, The Structure of Scientific Revolutions

This is the primary source behind Lecture 6. Two chapters were assigned: "The Route to Normal Science" and "The Resolution of Revolutions."

  • On paradigms: Kuhn stresses that a paradigm is a concrete achievement (like Newton's Principia or Lavoisier's Chemistry) that is sufficiently unprecedented to attract a lasting group of followers, yet sufficiently open-ended to leave plenty of problems for those followers to solve. Learning science means learning these shared exemplars, not just abstract rules — which is why members of a paradigm-based community rarely have to argue over fundamentals.

  • On why paradigm choice is hard (incommensurability), in more depth:

    • Competing paradigms disagree about which problems a good theory even needs to solve (e.g., Newtonian physics was resisted partly because, unlike Aristotelian/Cartesian physics, it didn't explain the cause of gravitational attraction — it just described it).
    • New paradigms reuse old vocabulary (mass, space, earth) but embed the terms in new relationships, so cross-paradigm communication is inherently partial — a "misunderstanding between the two competing schools," in Kuhn's phrase.
    • Proponents of rival paradigms, in Kuhn's famous claim, "practice their trades in different worlds" — not literally, but in the sense that the same observations don't carry the same significance for both sides.
    • Because paradigm change means overhauling the whole conceptual web at once (not correcting it fact-by-fact), the transition is like a gestalt switch — instantaneous, not gradual — and it cannot be forced by logic or a "crucial experiment" alone.

  • On how revolutions actually get resolved: Kuhn is skeptical of both pure verificationism and pure Popperian falsificationism as full descriptions of theory change. Instead, paradigm change happens through persuasion, not proof:

    • The new paradigm's ability to solve the old paradigm's crisis-provoking problems is the most persuasive argument — especially if it does so with striking new quantitative precision (e.g., Kepler's tables) or by predicting entirely new, unanticipated phenomena (e.g., the "white spot" predicted by Fresnel's wave theory of light).
    • But problem-solving success is never fully decisive on its own — early versions of new paradigms are usually crude and don't out-perform the old one everywhere.
    • Aesthetic considerations (a theory feeling "neater," "simpler," more elegant) matter too, especially for the first few converts who develop the new paradigm before it can win over everyone else on "hard" evidence.
    • In the end, individual scientists convert for a mix of reasons — some die-hards never convert at all — and the whole field shifts through a gradual redistribution of professional allegiance, not one single collective "aha."

Lecture 7: Science in Society

Core questions: Why does science have authority/trust in some contexts and not others? How has the relationship between science and society/politics changed?

Expertise

  • Expertise = skill or knowledge in a domain; it's a source of social power in modern societies, and access to it is usually gatekept through education and credentialing.
  • Jasanoff's definition (quoted in lecture): expertise today is really about "judgment in the face of uncertainty," and the ability to exercise that judgment in the public interest — not the possession of settled, certain facts.
  • Because expert judgment operates under uncertainty, errors are inevitable over time, and each error risks eroding public trust — which raises the hard question: when things go wrong, where does the fault actually lie (with the science, the scientist, the communication, or the institution)?

Case: the Aquila earthquake trial

  • Background context given in lecture: Giampaolo Giuliani (an amateur/independent researcher at the Istituto Nazionale di Fisica Nucleare) had predicted seismic activity before a deadly 2009 earthquake in L'Aquila, Italy. Government-appointed scientific experts publicly reassured the public that a major quake was unlikely shortly before it struck, killing over 300 people. Several scientists were controversially prosecuted (initially convicted, then largely acquitted on appeal) for the manner of their public risk communication.
  • Why it matters: it's a case where scientific uncertainty was communicated to the public as false certainty/reassurance — and the backlash exposed how much scientific authority depends on how experts communicate risk, not just on getting the underlying science "right."

Case: climate science

  • Used in lecture as the paradigm example of a scientific "controversy" that is really about the politicization of expertise: a well-established scientific consensus becomes a site of public/political dispute, showing how "settling" a technical question is never purely a matter of more data.

Case: Sheep farming in Cumbria after Chernobyl

(See the Collins & Pinch reading below — same case, used to make the same point: scientific expertise clashing with local/lay knowledge.)

Why (and how) do we trust science? Key theoretical framework

  • Problem of Legitimation vs. Problem of Extension (Collins & Evans, Rethinking Expertise):
    • The Problem of Legitimation asks: what justifies giving scientific expertise special authority over political/social decisions in the first place?
    • The Problem of Extension asks: once we grant that legitimacy, how far should it extend — who else's input (lay people, affected communities) should count, and where should the boundary of "who counts as an expert" be drawn?
    • These two pull in opposite directions: tightening the boundary (only "real" scientists count) protects legitimacy but risks excluding relevant local knowledge (worsening extension); loosening it (let more voices in) helps extension but can undercut the special authority that legitimizes science in the first place.
  • Science as autonomous vs. transparent: should science claim authority by staying independent/insulated from politics, or by being open/public and inviting scrutiny? Lecture frames this as a genuine tension, not a settled answer.
  • Epstein's point about "scientization of politics" / "politicization of science": increasingly, political and social controversies get converted into technical disputes so that science can "settle" them — but this means each side recruits its own experts, turning science itself into a political battleground (like dueling expert witnesses in a courtroom).
  • "Science as politics by other means": the line between science and society blurs — science doesn't just describe the world, it helps make social order, and its power rests partly on rhetoric and persuasion, not on logic/data alone. (This directly echoes Kuhn's point from Lecture 6 that paradigm change is a matter of persuasion, not pure proof — same idea, now applied to science's relationship with the public.)

The "post-1945 moment" (appears in both lectures)

After WWII, science's relationship to politics and power changed dramatically: the image of scientists was elevated, governments actively funded and directed research (WWII, Cold War), and new collaborative/large-scale ways of organizing science emerged — plus a new "social contract for science" (an implicit deal: society funds and trusts science; science delivers useful, reliable knowledge in return). This context helps explain why public trust in science became such a central, fraught issue in the postwar period — a contract that can be, and was, tested by cases like the sheep farmers and Aquila.

Big takeaway (ties directly to Lecture 6)

"Socially robust knowledge" isn't about scientific claims being logically/epistemically airtight — it's a layered achievement:

  • internal: how the scientific community itself manages disagreement and shares information
  • external: how the community manages its relationship to the wider public — including whether it respects lay/local expertise Knowledge that ignores the external, social dimension — however good the internal science — can still fail to be trusted or "robust" in practice.

Reading: Collins & Pinch, The Golem at Large, ch. 6 — "The Science of the Lambs: Chernobyl and the Cumbrian Sheepfarmers"

The story: After the 1986 Chernobyl disaster, radioactive fallout drenched the Cumbrian fells (a sheep-farming region in England) in an unusually heavy rainstorm. UK government scientists (via MAFF, the agriculture ministry) initially and confidently declared there was no health risk, predicting contamination would decline quickly and disappear within about a week or two. Instead, radioactive caesium levels in sheep kept rising for years. A short sales/movement ban became an indefinite one, eventually restricting around a fifth of the UK's sheep — devastating for farmers whose entire yearly income depends on precisely-timed lamb sales.

What went wrong scientifically: Scientists modeled the upland (peaty, acidic) Cumbrian soil using assumptions from lowland clay soil, where caesium quickly binds/locks up chemically. In upland peaty soil, caesium instead stays chemically mobile and keeps getting re-absorbed by grass roots — meaning the sheep were repeatedly re-contaminated, not just contaminated once. The right data (on caesium behavior in soils) had existed since the 1960s, but had only been read in terms of direct physical exposure risk, not the indirect chemical/biological uptake pathway that mattered here — a nice concrete illustration of theory-dependence of observation: the same old data looked irrelevant until a new question made it relevant.

Why trust broke down — this is the heart of the chapter's argument:

  1. False certainty, not honest uncertainty. Scientists and officials repeatedly declared confident, reassuring timelines ("over within a week or ten days") that then had to be reversed. Farmers didn't mind that scientists made mistakes (farmers are used to an uncertain world) — what damaged trust was the refusal to admit uncertainty or error, and the swagger with which wrong predictions were delivered.
  2. Dismissal of lay/local expertise. Farmers had deep practical knowledge — about where water/radiation would pool on their land, about the delicate, flexible timing needed to bring lambs to market at the right condition, about the fact that sheep would never eat imported straw, about how fencing sheep into experimental plots would cause them to "waste." Scientists ignored this knowledge (e.g., ran a fenced bentonite-mineral experiment the farmers said wouldn't work — and it later failed for exactly the reason the farmers gave) and imposed rigid, bureaucratic rules (like "just wait to sell" or "feed them straw") that showed no understanding of how hill farming actually works.
  3. Farmers directly witnessing the ordinary messiness of science. Watching scientists struggle to calibrate instruments, get inconsistent readings, and even literally re-measure sheep until fewer "failed" contamination tests, farmers saw firsthand the normal, open-ended, imperfect nature of real ("golem") science — which sharply contradicted the public face of certainty officials presented. (This is Collins & Pinch's core "golem" metaphor from the book as a whole: science is like a golem — powerful but clumsy, human-made, not perfect or infallible, and dangerous mainly when we expect too much certainty from it.)
  4. The "Sellafield factor" — conspiracy suspicion. The contamination "hot spots" coincidentally clustered near the Sellafield nuclear reprocessing plant, which had its own dark history (including a secretive 1957 reactor fire). Farmers suspected the "Chernobyl" story was covering up decades of unacknowledged local Sellafield contamination. Scientists tried to definitively rule this out using an isotope ratio "fingerprint" (caesium-137 vs. caesium-134, which decay at very different rates and can distinguish the two sources) — but farmers, having just watched official figures smooth over real, dramatic small-scale variability, reasonably doubted whether the "clean" isotope story was really as certain as claimed. (The chapter notes this suspicion was partly vindicated: later science confirmed a meaningful share of the caesium indeed came from other sources, including Sellafield and weapons testing, not just Chernobyl.)

The authors' diagnosis (ties directly back to Kuhn/lecture themes): Farmers ended up oscillating between two equally mistaken views of science — first "science = certainty" (which shattered when predictions failed), then, in overcorrection, "science = pure political conspiracy" serving government interests. Collins & Pinch argue both extremes are wrong. The healthier position (their "golem science" idea) is to recognize science as fallible, human, socially embedded work — good, useful, and worth trusting, but never infallible or context-free — and to actively value lay expertise alongside scientific expertise, rather than treating science as either omniscient authority or empty rhetoric.

Reading Questions — Model Answers

Q1. What does Kuhn mean by a "paradigm"? What kinds of things make it up? A paradigm is a whole shared framework of practice for a scientific community, anchored in one or more landmark achievements (exemplary solved problems) that the community treats as the model for future work. It's made up of: shared theories/laws and concepts; shared instruments and experimental techniques; agreed standards for what counts as a legitimate question and an adequate solution; and (more loosely) a shared worldview or "form of scientific life." It's bigger than just a "theory" — it's closer to the whole toolkit and mindset a trained community brings to normal, puzzle-solving science.

Q2. Why is it so hard to choose between two competing paradigms? What does "incommensurable" mean? It's hard because rival paradigms don't share a neutral, agreed-upon standard for comparison: they disagree about which problems even count as important to solve, key terms shift meaning across the divide (e.g., "mass," "earth"), and practitioners trained in different paradigms notice/value different things when looking at the very same phenomena — Kuhn's provocative claim that they "live in different worlds." Because switching paradigms means rebuilding the whole conceptual web at once rather than fixing it one fact at a time, the change functions more like a conversion or gestalt switch than a step-by-step logical deduction — no single "crucial experiment" can force it. "Incommensurable" captures exactly this: there's no common, external measuring stick to definitively rank the two paradigms against each other.

Q3. Why was it so hard for scientists to understand and listen to the shepherds? Partly a knowledge-pathway problem: scientists modeled Cumbrian upland soil on lowland-clay assumptions and simply hadn't anticipated the ongoing root-uptake pathway, so they didn't expect (or look for) the kind of long-term, hyper-local variability the farmers were already living with. But mostly it was an institutional/attitude problem: scientists and bureaucrats assumed their formal expertise made local, practical knowledge irrelevant, so they didn't ask farmers what they knew, dismissed their objections (even when farmers turned out to be right, e.g., about the bentonite experiment and about sheep never eating straw), and imposed one-size-fits-all bureaucratic rules onto a farming practice that actually depends on constant, flexible, on-the-ground judgment. Underlying it all was overconfidence: scientists spoke with public certainty that didn't match the messy, uncertain reality farmers watched them struggle with directly in the field.

Q4. Does reading Kuhn change how you see the Cumbrian sheep case? Yes — Kuhn helps explain why the scientists were so confident and slow to adjust, not just that they were. The scientists were working inside a settled "normal science" model of how caesium behaves in soil (a paradigm built on lowland-soil assumptions) and, per Kuhn, normal science treats anomalies as puzzles to be explained away within the existing framework, not as signs the framework itself is wrong — which is exactly why it took nearly two years of persistently high contamination (an accumulating "anomaly") before the model was revised. It also reframes the farmers' turn to "Sellafield conspiracy" thinking: Kuhn's point that data never speaks for itself, and that theories are always underdetermined by data, helps explain why the isotope "fingerprint" argument didn't automatically settle the dispute for the farmers — they had independent (if informal) reasons to doubt the specific interpretation being offered, much like rival-paradigm scientists talking past each other. In short, Kuhn suggests this wasn't just a story about arrogant scientists, but a structural feature of how any established framework (scientific or lay) resists revision until anomalies pile up.

Likely Cold-Call Questions (Instructor Style) — With Model Answers

Q: In one sentence, what's the difference between logical positivism and falsificationism? A: Positivism says theories are built up from and verified by empirical data; falsificationism (Popper) says theories can never be verified, only tested and potentially falsified, so good science is bold, risky theorizing that survives serious attempts to refute it.

Q: What is underdetermination, and why does it matter more than "does the theory fit the data"? A: Underdetermination means the available data are compatible with more than one theory, so data alone can never force a unique choice of theory — meaning the deeper scientific problem is how communities choose among data-compatible rivals (via other factors: simplicity, community judgment, predictive novelty, even aesthetics), not just whether a theory "fits."

Q: How does Kuhn's idea of "normal science" explain why scientists are slow to abandon a paradigm even when anomalies appear? A: In normal science, an unsolved puzzle is normally blamed on the scientist's technique or a fixable auxiliary assumption, not on the paradigm itself — the whole point of a paradigm is that it's assumed to work, so anomalies accumulate for a long time (creating a "crisis") before the community seriously entertains replacing the paradigm.

Q: Give a concrete example from the readings of "theory-dependence of observation." A: In the Cumbrian case, decades-old data on caesium's chemical behavior in upland soils already existed, but scientists had only ever read that data through the lens of "how deep is caesium buried, and how much direct radiation exposure does that cause" — they didn't see it as relevant to a completely different question (root uptake and re-contamination of grass) until the sheep crisis forced a new interpretive framework onto the same old data.

Q: What's the "Problem of Legitimation" vs. the "Problem of Extension," and why is it a genuine dilemma rather than something you can just "solve"? A: Legitimation asks what justifies giving scientific expertise special decision-making authority; Extension asks how far that authority (or who else's knowledge) should be allowed to count. It's a real dilemma because policies that strengthen one tend to weaken the other — drawing a tight boundary around "real" science protects its special authority (legitimation) but shuts out potentially valuable local/lay knowledge (hurting extension); opening the boundary to more voices helps extension but risks diluting the very authority that made science trustworthy in the first place.

Q: How does the Cumbrian sheepfarmers case illustrate "socially robust knowledge" (or its failure)? A: The scientific claims may have eventually become technically accurate, but the process failed the "social" half of robust knowledge: officials communicated false certainty, dismissed farmers' locally-grounded practical expertise, and imposed rules that ignored how hill farming actually works — so even correct science, delivered badly and without respecting local knowledge, produced a lasting collapse of public trust rather than "robust" accepted knowledge.

Q: What does Collins & Pinch mean by calling science a "golem"? A: A golem (from Jewish folklore, referenced throughout their book) is a powerful, human-made creature that is clumsy and imperfect, not evil but not omniscient either — the metaphor for science is that it is a genuinely powerful and valuable human creation, but fallible, unfinished, and shaped by the ordinary messiness of the people who make it, so we shouldn't expect (or demand) infallible certainty from it.

Q: Was the Aquila earthquake case fundamentally a failure of science or a failure of science communication? A: The lecture frames it more as a failure of communicating uncertainty as false certainty — the underlying seismological science genuinely could not precisely predict the quake (that's expected uncertainty), but public reassurances were delivered as if the risk were confidently low, which is what triggered the backlash and legal fallout once the quake occurred; it's a case study in how expert judgment under uncertainty, and how that judgment gets communicated, is itself part of what determines whether "expertise" is trusted afterward.

Q: How do Lecture 6 and Lecture 7 fit together as one argument? A: Lecture 6 (via Kuhn) establishes that scientific knowledge is never just "read off" neutral data — it's always shaped by community-held paradigms, assumptions, and social/historical context. Lecture 7 then applies that same insight to science's relationship with the public: if even scientists-among-themselves don't have a purely neutral, logical basis for their knowledge, then science's authority with the public also can't rest on claims of pure, certain, apolitical truth — it has to be built and maintained socially, through trust, communication, and respect for other forms of knowledge (like the farmers'), which is what "socially robust knowledge" means.

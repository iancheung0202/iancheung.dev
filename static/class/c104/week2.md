# Warmup 2 Study Guide: Classification, Data, and Making People

## 1. Bowker & Star, *Sorting Things Out* — Ch. 6: "Race Classification and Reclassification under Apartheid"

**Big picture:** Classification systems aren't neutral bookkeeping — they're sites of political struggle that get buried under a technical/bureaucratic surface. Apartheid South Africa is the extreme case study of what happens when a state tries to force a messy human reality (race) into rigid legal boxes.

**Key concept — "Torque":** The twisting/damage that happens to a person's life story (biography) when a classification system doesn't match their actual lived experience, appearance, relationships, or location. The system stays rigid; the person's life gets bent/torn to fit it (or doesn't fit, and they suffer for it).

**Key concept — Aristotelian vs. Prototypical categories:**

- *Aristotelian* = categories with hard, logical, necessary-and-sufficient boundaries (the law's fantasy: "White," "Coloured," "Bantu," "Asiatic" as clean boxes).
- *Prototypical* = categories based on fuzzy resemblance, "best examples," and everyday judgment (what a policeman or tram conductor *sees* and *feels* is normal).
- Apartheid's core contradiction: the law demanded Aristotelian precision but could only ever be enforced using prototypical, subjective judgment (appearance, "general acceptance," hair texture, etc.). This conflation is where all the horror and absurdity comes from.

**Mechanics of the system:**

- **1950 Population Registration Act + Group Areas Act**: everyone must be classified into 4 basic races (European, Asiatic, Coloured, Bantu/Native), which then determined where you could live, work, go to school, marry, etc.
- **Pass books ("reference books")**: required ID document for Black South Africans, 50+ pages, tracked birth, tax, employment, etc. — total surveillance infrastructure.
- **Reclassification Boards**: heard appeals from people who didn't fit their assigned category. Criteria were literally "appearance and general acceptance and repute" — not blood/descent (explicitly rejecting the American "one-drop rule," since almost all White South Africans had some Black ancestry).
- **"Technologies of classifying"**: the *pencil test* (pencil stuck in hair — if it falls out, "white/coloured" enough; if it stays, "too woolly," you're Black), examining earlobes, cheekbones, skin-color scales (Fig 6.3), interrogating relatives, checking if you eat porridge or sleep on a bed vs. the floor.
- Between coloured/white reclassification, coloured→white approvals vastly outnumbered white→coloured (moving "down" was easier than moving "up").

**Key examples to remember:**

- **Vic Wilkinson**: jazz musician reclassified across race lines *five times* in his life as he remarried and moved.
- **Sandra Laing**: born to white parents, expelled from her white school at age 10 for looking coloured; later reclassified white again, but never socially accepted; eventually lived with a Black man and asked to be reclassified again so she could live with him legally. Symbol of how legal classification and social/informal racism can contradict each other.
- **David Wong**: Chinese man whose White neighbors gave affidavits calling him "White" under the "general acceptance" clause — used almost as an act of protest — which triggered a government crackdown/amendment.
- **"Dottie"** and the **Griqua people**: cases where language and appearance clashed with assigned race, leaving people unable to attend any appropriate school.
- **Ronnie van der Walt**: boxer suddenly reclassified from white to coloured right before a big match — a targeted, humiliating "lesson" to others.

**Author's larger point:** This is an extreme case, but *all* information systems that attach categories to people (age brackets, user profiles, census race categories, etc.) can produce this same "torque." The chapter closes by comparing it to the 1990s US census debate over allowing people to check multiple racial boxes — same underlying problem of imposing rigid categories on people who don't fit neatly (they call this "strategic essentialism" when groups defend an imperfect category because it's useful for claiming resources/rights).

## 2. Ian Hacking — "Making Up People" (LRB, 2006)

**Big picture / core idea — "Making up people":** Sometimes the sciences (especially the "human sciences": psychology, psychiatry, sociology, clinical medicine) don't just *discover* pre-existing kinds of people — they actually *create new ways of being a person* that didn't exist before. This isn't mystical; it means people start to experience themselves, and interact with others, through a new category (e.g., "the multiple personality," "the high-functioning autist").

**Key concept — "The Looping Effect":** Classifications aren't static targets. When you classify and study people, they *change* in response to being classified (they learn the label, act it out, seek the diagnosis, community forms around it). Then the classification has to update to match the changed people. It's a feedback loop between the category and the people in it — the target keeps moving.

**Key distinction — Statement A vs. Statement B** (Hacking's central logical move):

- (A) "There were no [X] in 1955; there were many in 1985." — a claim about *existence*, often unresolvable/pointless to argue about.
- (B) "In 1955 this wasn't a way to be a person... in 1985 it was a way to be a person, to experience oneself, to live in society." — a claim about *social roles/experience*, which is what Hacking actually means by "making up people." Both skeptics and believers about a diagnosis can agree on B even if they fight about A.
- Example: Multiple Personality Disorder (MPD, now Dissociative Identity Disorder) — A is (he argues) true; B is definitely true.
- Contrast example: High-functioning autism — A is false (autistic people surely existed in 1950), but B is still true (this specific *way of being a person* — with community, self-understanding, advocacy — didn't exist yet).

**Case study — Multiple Personality Disorder:**

- 1970s: a few sensational diagnosed cases → number of "alters" per patient kept rising (avg. went from 2-3 to 17 within a decade) → symptoms fed back into diagnostic criteria → talk shows (Oprah, Geraldo) spread it → "split bars" appeared by 1991.
- Hacking's 5-part framework for "making up people": (a) the classification/label, (b) the people themselves, (c) institutions (clinics, conferences, talk shows), (d) the knowledge (facts, causal theories — even if later disputed), (e) the experts who produce and apply the knowledge.

**The Ten "Engines" of making up people** (memorize the logic, not just the list):

1. **Count!** – just counting a population as a group creates visibility (e.g., autism prevalence surveys).
2. **Quantify!** – turning something into a number (BMI, IQ).
3. **Create Norms!** – defining what's "normal" automatically defines deviance (Canguilhem).
4. **Correlate!** – statistical correlation (Galton) links traits to categories, sometimes spuriously.
5. **Medicalise!** – turn a behavior into a medical condition (child abuse, suicide, obesity).
6. **Biologise!** – find a bodily/neurological basis (removes moral blame — e.g., "it's not weak will, it's biology").
7. **Geneticise!** – push further to genetic causes (criminality, homosexuality "gay gene" search).
8. **Normalise!** – treatments/therapies designed to bring people "back to normal" (behavioral therapy, anti-craving drugs).
9. **Bureaucratise!** – institutions build systems (school special-ed screening) that in turn define who counts as belonging to the category.
10. **Resist! ("Reclaim our identity")** – the classified group pushes back and reclaims control of their own label (gay pride; autism self-advocacy; "Health at Every Size" for obesity).

**Natural Kinds (John Stuart Mill's distinction):**

- A "real kind" (e.g., *horse*) has tons of properties bundled together beyond the defining one.
- A "finite kind" (e.g., *white things*) shares only the one defining property and nothing else.
- Hacking applies this to argue autism is closer to a "real kind" (bundle of many correlated traits/behaviors, part of someone's whole nature) while obesity is more of a "finite kind" (people share only being overweight, nothing else necessarily connects them) — this is why "autism is who my son is" makes sense as a statement but "obesity is who I am" usually doesn't.

**Other examples used:** Suicide (medicalized since ~1825, now tied to depression — makes it hard to think clearly about suicide bombing or euthanasia); Genius (once Romantic and un-measurable, now IQ-tested into oblivion — "genius abhors a measure").

## 3. Lecture 3 — "Making Data"

Intro lecture — mostly course logistics + framing. Key ideas to retain:

- Data is *made*, not just collected — every dataset reflects choices about what to count, how to categorize, and who decides.
- Introduces the theme that classification and representation are never neutral — sets up the whole course arc (Data → Personal Data → People-from-Data).
- Look at your own slide images for specific visual examples shown (census forms, historical photos) if the instructor references them, but no unique arguments beyond framing.

## 4. Lecture 4 — "Making Personal Data"

Builds toward personal/individual data collection. Key themes:

- **Punch cards at Berkeley (1960s)**: early symbol of student data being reduced to standardized categories on a card — controversial (student protest imagery, "Do Not Fold, Spindle, or Mutilate").
- **Classification and social order**: classification isn't just descriptive — it actively organizes society (who goes where, who gets what).
- **Circulation and durability**: once data is recorded, it travels and persists beyond its original context — this is a source of vulnerability (data can be reused against the person later).
- **Reflexivity**: the act of measuring/representing a person can change their behavior (a preview of performativity).
- **Vulnerability**: personal data creates risk precisely because of its durability and circulation — once made, it's hard to control where it goes or how it's used.
- **What is personal data?** — introduces working definition/framework questions (what counts, how it's regulated, who owns it) as a springboard into Lecture 5.

## 5. Lecture 5 — "Making People Out of Data" (this is the lecture built directly on Hacking + Butler)

**Core question of the lecture:** What does it mean to say we "make people out of data"? How do representations/classifications *bring about* or *shape* the very people/phenomena they claim to just describe?

**Key concept — Performativity** (this is the central vocabulary word of the whole course unit):

> "The way that actions that describe the world (language, concepts, metaphors, models, classification systems, measurement systems, predictions, automated decision-making systems) can shape and even make the very phenomena they set out to describe."

**What performativity is NOT** (important — instructor will likely test this distinction):

- It does **not** mean "something worked well/functioned as expected" (a machine "performing" its task).
- It does **not** mean expressing an inner authentic state ("I performed my true feelings").
- It does **not** mean "pretending"/acting/fakeness (like "performative allyship" as an insult).
- Correct meaning: language/classification is a form of *action* that brings about new social realities — like saying "I do" at a wedding actually creates a marriage; it doesn't just describe one.

**Judith Butler's contribution:** Gender identity is an *effect*, not a cause, of repeated gender performances — you don't have an inner gender essence that performances express; the performances (styled repetition of acts) *produce* the appearance of an essential gendered self.

**Connecting Hacking + Butler:** Classifications (race, gender, mental health diagnoses like MPD) that get practiced, enforced, discussed, and internalized can establish entirely new ways of being a self. Example: "introversion" wasn't a meaningful way to be a person before that psychological classification existed.

**Personality as a historical product, not a timeless fact:**

- Before ~1900s: people were understood through "character" (moral virtue/vice), not measurable "personality."
- "Personality" became a *bundle of measurable, describable traits* starting around 1917, tied to: psychology as a discipline, new measurement/testing practices, and industrial organization (need to sort/manage large workforces — think early personality tests, IQ tests, punch cards).
- Example figures: Robert Sessions Woodworth's "Personal Data Sheet" (WWI-era personality test).

**"Know thyself" and self-quantification:**

- Ancient idea (Delphic maxim) that self-knowledge requires external tools: writing, counting, mirrors — these *externalize and objectify* the self so it can be examined.
- Modern versions: personality quizzes (Buzzfeed/Myers-Briggs-style), the **Quantified Self** movement (Fitbit, Apple Watch, self-tracking apps), and even historical precedents like Benjamin Franklin's *Book of Virtues* (13-virtue self-tracking chart) and Santorio Santorio (1561–1636), who literally weighed himself and his food/waste daily — an early "quantified self."
- Contemporary/extreme example: **"Looksmaxxing"** — using facial measurement/self-tracking apps to rate and try to optimize one's physical appearance.

**Cyborg concept:**

- "Cyborg" = cybernetic organism; a metaphor (from Clynes & Kline, 1960) for how technology and biology blur into each other, breaking down boundaries like human/animal, machine/organism, physical/non-physical.
- **Colin Koopman quote** (key one to remember): "Our data are not mere externalia attached to us from which we might detach our truer selves as we please, but are rather constitutive parts of who we can be... We are cyborgs who extend into our data."
- Point: your data isn't just information *about* you sitting outside you — it's part of *constructing* who you are.

**Key Takeaways (from the lecture's own summary slide — good to just memorize directly):**

1. The "self" is *made*, not simply found — it's produced together with technology and society, so it's contingent/variable, not fixed.
2. Quantified self-tracking is one specific technique by which the self gets "made" in a datafied world.
3. Ways of being a self/person are products of the *performativity* of classifications, sociotechnical systems, norms, and practices.
4. The "cyborg" is a useful figure for thinking of selfhood as porous and relational — built from personal, social, biological, and technical elements together.

**Diagnostic questions the slides explicitly give you** (very likely source of exam/discussion questions — memorize these as a checklist you can apply to ANY case study, including apartheid, MPD, autism, personality tests):

- What does a representation or prediction **do** in the world? How does it bring about new states of affairs?
- What assumptions are being naturalized along the way?
- How does a classification system/representation/narrative/technology create and impose identities on individuals and populations?
- How does it affect people's agency and well-being? Whose agency or power does it serve?
- What makes this knowledge *productive* (i.e., generative of new realities, not just descriptive)?
- How does performativity help knowledge serve social control?

## How the Readings Connect (the through-line)

| Reading | What it shows about classification |
| --- | --- |
| Bowker & Star (Apartheid) | Classification imposed by *state power* creates massive, violent "torque" in people's lives; shows the impossible mixing of rigid legal categories with fuzzy real-world judgment. |
| Hacking (Making Up People) | Classification by the *human sciences* (psychiatry, psychology) doesn't just describe people — it creates genuinely new ways of being a person via the "looping effect." |
| Lecture 5 / Butler | Generalizes both into the concept of **performativity**: classifications/representations are actions that *produce* the realities and identities they claim to merely record. |
| Quantified Self / Personality tests | Shows performativity at the *individual, voluntary* level — self-tracking data doesn't just record who you are, it actively shapes who you become. |

**One unifying thesis you could give in class:** Classification systems — whether imposed by a violent state (apartheid), by expert institutions (psychiatry), or adopted voluntarily (self-tracking apps) — are never just neutral descriptions of a pre-existing reality. They are *actions* that create, enforce, and reshape the very people and identities they claim to describe (performativity), and because classifications are rigid while human lives are messy, this always produces some degree of strain, harm, or "torque" for people who don't fit cleanly.

## Practice Q&A (likely instructor questions)

**Q: What is "torque," and give an example.**
A: Torque (Bowker & Star) is the damage/distortion done to a person's biography when a classification system doesn't match their actual life. Example: Sandra Laing, born to white parents but classified coloured due to appearance, expelled from her white school at 10 — her legal category and her lived/social reality kept clashing throughout her life.

**Q: What's the difference between Aristotelian and prototypical categories, and why does apartheid law fail because of this?**
A: Aristotelian categories have sharp, all-or-nothing definitions; prototypical categories rely on fuzzy resemblance and everyday judgment. Apartheid law wanted Aristotelian precision (four fixed races) but could only be enforced through prototypical judgments (a policeman's glance, the pencil test, "general acceptance"). This mismatch is why the system produced constant absurdity and cruelty — it demanded certainty about something inherently fuzzy.

**Q: Explain Hacking's "looping effect" with an example.**
A: Classifying and studying people changes them, which then changes the classification, in an ongoing feedback loop. Example: as MPD (Multiple Personality Disorder) became more publicized, patients' number of "alters" rose from 2–3 to an average of 17 within a decade — the diagnosis shaped how people experienced and presented their disorder, which then reshaped the diagnostic criteria.

**Q: What does "performativity" mean, and what are two things it's often confused with?**
A: Performativity means language/classification/measurement systems don't just describe the world — they help *bring about* the very realities they describe (like saying "I do" creates a marriage). It's commonly and incorrectly confused with (1) a system simply "performing" its function well, and (2) "pretending" or fakeness (as in "performative allyship").

**Q: How does Judith Butler's account of gender relate to Hacking's "making up people"?**
A: Both argue identity is an *effect* produced by repeated actions/classifications rather than an essence that precedes them. Butler argues gender is produced by repeated performances, not expressed by them. Hacking argues that categories like "multiple personality" or "high-functioning autistic" create genuinely new ways of experiencing selfhood that didn't exist as social roles before the classification did.

**Q: Distinguish Hacking's Statement A from Statement B, using autism as the example.**
A: (A) "There were no high-functioning autists in 1950; there were many in 2000" — Hacking says this is false, since autistic people with these traits certainly existed. (B) "In 1950 this wasn't a way to be a person... in 2000 it was" — this is true: the social role, community, and self-understanding of being "a high-functioning autistic person" genuinely didn't exist as an available identity before the diagnostic category matured.

**Q: What are "natural kinds" and how does Hacking use Mill's distinction?**
A: Mill distinguished "real kinds" (things sharing many bundled properties, like *horse*) from "finite kinds" (things sharing only the one defining property, like *white things*). Hacking uses this to argue autism functions more like a real kind (a bundle of correlated traits that's part of a person's whole nature — "autism is who my son is") while obesity functions more like a finite kind (people share only their weight, nothing deeper necessarily connects them).

**Q: Why does the lecture bring up the Quantified Self movement and looksmaxxing?**
A: To show performativity operating at the individual/voluntary level — self-tracking doesn't just record facts about you, it actively shapes your self-understanding and behavior (what you count becomes what you optimize for, and your sense of self becomes bound up with the metrics).

**Q: What is Koopman's "cyborg" argument, and why does it matter for this unit?**
A: Koopman argues our data isn't external information *about* a "truer self" hiding somewhere else — data is a *constitutive part* of who we are ("we are cyborgs who extend into our data"). This matters because it pushes back against the idea that a person exists fully formed prior to and separate from the data/classifications applied to them — reinforcing the whole unit's argument that selfhood is made, not just found.

**Q: Apply the lecture's "diagnostic questions" to the apartheid case.**
A: What did racial classification *do*? It restricted where people could live, work, marry, attend school. What assumptions were naturalized? That race is a fixed, visually-legible biological fact rather than a social/political construct. Whose power did it serve? The white Nationalist minority's control over land, labor, and social status. What made this knowledge "productive"? It generated an entire bureaucratic-legal infrastructure (pass books, reclassification boards) that structured everyday life and enforced racial hierarchy under the appearance of technical, administrative neutrality.
